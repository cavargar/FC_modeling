## 1. Laboratory methods (pressure plate/extractor at −10/−33 kPa, sandbox/tension table): biases and agreement with field FC

### Takeaway
In the lab, FC is taken as θ at a fixed matric potential: −33 kPa is the US convention and −10 kPa is common in Europe and New Zealand (−5 to −20 kPa also appears). Field-based studies repeatedly find that a single fixed suction is a biased proxy for FC. On USDA-ARS data, θ(−33 kPa) underestimated measured field FC by about 0.08 cm³/cm³ and correlated poorly with it. In Brazilian soils, θ(−6 kPa) matched field FC best. Pressure-plate equilibrium errors are documented mostly at the dry end (below about −200 kPa) and in fine-textured soils, so they matter more for wilting point than for FC. They are not negligible for fine soils, though.

### Cited Findings
- **Suction conventions.** FC is commonly measured in the lab at −10 kPa, some studies use −5 to −20 kPa, and −33 kPa is typical in the USA — [NZ Ministry for the Environment, "Soil water storage capacity and fluxes"](https://environment.govt.nz/assets/publications/Environmental-Reporting/7.10-Soil-water-storage-capacity-and-fluxes.pdf) (snippet).
- An open soil physics textbook (Oklahoma State, *Rain or Shine*) says recent research shows −33 kPa is "a poor approximation of field capacity in most cases", and that less negative values (−10 or even −6 kPa) look more suitable — [Rain or Shine, 8.3 Field Capacity](https://open.library.okstate.edu/rainorshine/chapter/8-3-field-capacity/) (snippet).
- **θ33 vs field FC, quantified (USA).** Pachepsky, Nemes & Hamilton (2008 ASA meeting poster) used USDA-ARS data from 30 US locations. θ33 gave a biased representation of FC, with FC larger than θ33 by about **0.08 cm³/cm³**, and θ33 correlated poorly with FC. Regression trees were then used to predict FC directly from soil properties — [Pachepsky et al. 2008, ASA-CSSA-SSSA abstract](https://scisoc.confex.com/crops/2008am/webprogram/Paper44656.html) (abstract).
- Nemes, Pachepsky & Timlin (2010, 19th WCSS) used regression trees with jack-knife cross-validation, taking sand, silt, clay and measured W33 as inputs to correct W33 toward FC. FC estimates improved "in general statistical terms" and **texture-specific bias was greatly reduced** — [Nemes et al. 2010, 19th WCSS](https://old.iuss.org/19th%20WCSS/Symposium/pdf/0021.pdf) (abstract). The later paper (Nemes, Pachepsky & Timlin 2011) is cited for the view that FC "usually measured at 10 or 33 kPa… is generally considered incompatible with the definition of FC" — [Embrapa 2016 paper citing it](https://www.alice.cnptia.embrapa.br/alice/bitstream/doc/1051668/1/2016026.pdf) (snippet).
- **θ6 vs field FC (Brazil, 29 profiles).** Water contents at 6, 10 and 33 kPa were compared with in situ FC measured as θ after 48 h of drainage. θ(6 kPa) correlated best. A quadratic PTF on θ6 gave a global RMSR of **0.026 m³/m³** and beat the classical "FC = θ at 6, 10 or 33 kPa" assumption. The 48-h time was chosen partly because small deep-percolation rates are hard to measure experimentally — [Embrapa (Alice repository) 2016](https://www.alice.cnptia.embrapa.br/alice/bitstream/doc/1051668/1/2016026.pdf) (snippet). This is tropical/subtropical data; see the scope note.
- **Lab 10/33 kPa vs in situ (India).** In situ FC (θ once drainage from prewetted soil had practically ceased) was compared with lab retention at 10 and 33 kPa on core, clod, crushed and sieved samples. Correlation was significant (**r ≥ 0.9**) with either suction, and regression equations were derived to refine lab estimates — [Indian J. Agric. Sci., "Upper limit of available water in field as related to laboratory-determined parameters"](https://epubs.icar.org.in/index.php/IJAgS/article/view/30114) (abstract). High r with systematic offset suggests a bias that can be calibrated out.
- **Pressure-plate equilibration error (dry end).** Bittelli & Flury (2009, SSSAJ 73:1453–1460) found that pressure-plate retention on a silt loam diverged from dew-point (WP4) data below about −20 m H₂O (≈ −200 kPa), with plates giving *higher* water contents. Causes were low plate/soil conductance, poor plate–soil contact and soil dispersion. They suggest combining plates with dew-point measurements — [Bittelli & Flury 2009 record](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Errors-in-Water-Retention-Curves-Determined/99900547678601842); [EGU2009 abstract](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU2009/EGU2009-2062.pdf) (abstract).
- Solone, Bittelli, Tomei & Morari (2012, J. Hydrol.) found that Stackman sand-box/tension tables and pressure plates differed from dew-point retention for **fine-textured soils but not coarse ones**, and these errors propagated into lower simulated drainage and evaporation — [FAO AGRIS record, "Errors in water retention curves determined with pressure plates: effects on the soil water balance"](https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/65de57820f3e94b9e5cdde1b) (abstract).
- A Brazilian study (RBCS) questions the assumption of hydrostatic equilibrium between samples and plates. Sample pressure heads were *less negative* than the applied pressure, so water content was overestimated in the dry range, especially in fine-textured samples — [Rev. Bras. Ciênc. Solo, "Hydrostatic equilibrium between soil samples and pressure plates… consequences of a questionable assumption"](https://www.rbcsjournal.org/pt-br/article/hydrostatic-equilibrium-between-soil-samples-and-pressure-plates-used-in-soil-water-retention-determination-consequences-of-a-questionable-assumption) (abstract).
- **Method choice matters (2025).** An *Irrigation Science* 2025 paper found that pressure-plate and other methods gave significantly different FC and available-water values. Pressure plates were judged more suitable for fine-textured soils when a conservative threshold is wanted — [Irrig. Sci. 2025, s00271-025-01058-1](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s00271-025-01058-1) (snippet).
- **Static vs flux-based definition.** Meyer & Gee (1999, ASCE J. Geotech. Geoenviron. Eng.) define FC as "the water content at which the drainage flux from a soil ceases, or becomes negligible". They note that the common practice of taking θ at about 1/3 bar "ignores its flux-based nature" — [Meyer & Gee 1999, "Flux-Based Estimation of Field Capacity"](https://ascelibrary.com/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%291090-0241%281999%29125%3A7%28595%29) (abstract).

### Inferences
- The fixed-suction FC bias has a sign that depends on texture and dataset. Pachepsky et al. (2008) found θ33 *too low* versus field FC on US soils, which points to a less negative suction (−10 or −6 kPa) as more representative. That is consistent with the Brazilian θ6 result and the textbook statement. The Nemes et al. regression-tree correction indicates the bias is texture-dependent, so no single suction is right for all soils.
- Pressure-plate equilibration problems are best documented below about −200 kPa. For FC at −10/−33 kPa the larger error sources are probably (i) the choice of suction and (ii) disturbed vs undisturbed sample structure, not plate equilibration. In fine soils, though, a slow approach to equilibrium can still bias θ upward. This inference comes from the reported pressure range of the errors.
- Lab FC can be strongly correlated with field FC (r ≥ 0.9) yet still offset. Site- or texture-specific regression corrections (Indian study; Nemes et al.) are a cheap fix when a few field FC values are available.

### Gaps
- **Disturbed vs undisturbed samples.** The Indian study compared core, clod, crushed and sieved samples, but I could not get its effect sizes. Background knowledge (UNVERIFIED): sieved/repacked samples usually *underestimate* θ at low suctions (−10/−33 kPa) in structured soils because inter-aggregate pores are destroyed, and can overestimate it in coarse soils because of repacking density. A quantified source is needed.
- **Sandbox/tension table accuracy.** I found no quantitative accuracy statement specific to sand boxes or tension tables at −1 to −10 kPa beyond the Solone et al. (2012) fine-vs-coarse finding.
- Gee et al. (2002, Vadose Zone J. 1:172–178, "Hydraulic equilibrium in pressure plates"; doi:10.2136/vzj2002.1720) is the classic paper on non-equilibrium at −1500 kPa. I did not retrieve it. UNVERIFIED: it reports that pressure plates may never reach equilibrium at high suction and overestimate θ.
- I could not retrieve full numeric results of Bittelli & Flury (2009) for −33 kPa.

## 2. Field methods: ponding/drainage tests, internal drainage tests, timing conventions (24–72 h), and instruments

### Takeaway
The field reference is a drainage test. The soil is pre-wetted, usually by ponding or heavy rain, then covered to stop evaporation. FC is read once drainage becomes "negligible", typically 24–48 h later (sometimes up to 72 h, or 1–10 days in continuous-sensor analyses). Timing depends strongly on texture and layering. The test is labor-intensive and site-specific. Because "negligible" has no agreed threshold, FC from field tests also depends on the operator, the protocol and the sensor.

### Cited Findings
- **Timing convention (24–48 h).** FC "is typically measured 24–48 h after heavy rainfall or irrigation under natural drainage conditions". Liu et al. used a **natural-rainfall method**: events with >80 mm rain were selected, the soil was assumed to reach FC 24–48 h afterward, and FC was the average of daily (24 h) soil water content from an Insentek multi-depth capacitance-type probe. Six replicates per layer gave FC of 29.0–40.65% (tea garden) and 22.5–28.55% (grassland) over 20–100 cm — [Liu2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/agronomy15081973) (local file, read in full).
- **48 h in a tropical field study.** A 48-h drainage time was used partly because small deep-percolation rates are experimentally hard to measure — [Embrapa 2016](https://www.alice.cnptia.embrapa.br/alice/bitstream/doc/1051668/1/2016026.pdf) (snippet).
- **"One overnight drainage" convention for sands.** For well-drained, rapidly permeable Florida sands and gravelly loam, expert analysts treated FC as reached "after one night of drainage". FC was the θ just before the next scheduled irrigation (06:00 or 07:00) after a night with no inputs between 22:00 and irrigation time — [Bean2018](https://doi.org/10.2136/vzj2018.04.0073) (local file).
- **USDA-NRCS practice.** θFC "can be determined by visual review of soil drying in SMS records after large irrigation or precipitation events and managed as a temporally constant value for a soil" — USDA-NRCS (2005) National Engineering Handbook Part 652 Ch. 9, as cited in [Bean2018](https://doi.org/10.2136/vzj2018.04.0073).
- **Stabilization time varies 1–10 days.** In automated analysis of Oklahoma Mesonet data, the "stable period" after a peak taken as FC "may occur between 1 and 10 days after peak soil water content", depending on texture — [OSU Extension fact sheet, "Determining Field Capacity Using Continuous Soil Water Content Data"](https://extension.okstate.edu/fact-sheets/determining-field-capacity-using-continuous-soil-water-content-data.html) (snippet).
- **No standardized method.** "There is no standardized method for estimating field capacity and measuring it accurately can be challenging". Soil water content readings, and FC estimated from them, "can vary depending on the type of sensor used" — [OSU Extension fact sheet](https://extension.okstate.edu/fact-sheets/determining-field-capacity-using-continuous-soil-water-content-data) (snippet).
- **No universal drainage threshold.** "A universally accepted threshold value for soil water flow rate to be negligible has not been determined yet". The flux-based definition fixes FC at "an arbitrary fixed threshold of free drainage flux" — snippets surfaced with the [Embrapa 2016](https://www.alice.cnptia.embrapa.br/alice/bitstream/doc/1051668/1/2016026.pdf) and related results. Attribution of the first quote to a specific review is uncertain.
- **Layering effects.** Zettl et al. (2011, Can. J. Soil Sci. 91:133–147, "Influence of textural layering on field capacity of coarse soils") is cited for FC varying with depth because of heterogeneity — cited in [Liu2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/agronomy15081973) (ref. 49).
- **Sensors in the primary dataset.** Bean et al. used capacitance/FDR-type sensors at hourly time steps: Decagon ECH2O EC-5 at 7.6 cm in turf, and a Campbell Scientific sensor (listed as "SC161") at 15.2 cm in citrus. They cite Rosenbaum et al. (2011) for temperature and EC effects on ECH2O permittivity readings as a source of invalid cycles — [Bean2018](https://doi.org/10.2136/vzj2018.04.0073).
- **Sensor technologies.** Topp (2003, Hydrol. Process.) and Evett & Parkin (2005, VZJ) review soil water content sensing. Blonquist et al. (2005, J. Hydrol.) describe a time-domain transmission sensor "with TDR performance characteristics" — all cited in [Bean2018](https://doi.org/10.2136/vzj2018.04.0073).

### Inferences
- Field FC is a *process outcome*. It depends on wetting depth and amount (>80 mm in Liu2025, "large events" in NRCS guidance), on time since wetting, on evaporation control, on layering and on the sensor. A "24 h vs 48 h vs 72 h" choice can therefore shift FC by a texture-dependent amount. In sands (Bean2018) one night is enough; in loams and clays drainage keeps going for days (OSU 1–10 days).
- Comparing instruments: TDR, FDR/capacitance and TDT give θ directly at high frequency, which suits time-series FC algorithms. Neutron probes give accurate, calibrated θ over a large volume but only manually and episodically, which suits classic internal-drainage tests. Tensiometers give ψ, not θ. Paired with θ sensors, they give in situ retention points, so FC can be expressed as a matric potential and checked against −10/−33 kPa lab values. This framing is synthesized from the cited sensor reviews and general practice. See Gaps for unverified accuracy figures.

### Gaps
- **Internal drainage test protocol.** Hillel, Krentos & Stylianou (1972, Soil Sci. 114:395–400) and the Cassel & Nielsen (1986) / Romano & Santini (2002) chapters in *Methods of Soil Analysis* are the standard references. I could not retrieve them. UNVERIFIED protocol details: pond a plot (e.g., several m²) until the profile is wetted to depth, cover it with plastic to stop evaporation, monitor θ (neutron probe/TDR) and ψ (tensiometers) at several depths over days to weeks, and take FC where dθ/dt falls below a threshold or at a fixed time (often 2–3 days).
- **Instrument accuracy (UNVERIFIED).** Factory-calibrated capacitance sensors are often quoted at ±0.03–0.05 cm³/cm³, soil-specific calibration about ±0.01–0.02, and TDR about ±0.01–0.02. Neutron probes need site calibration and have a sphere of influence of about 15–30 cm. These figures need a primary source, e.g., Evett et al. 2012 or the Evett & Parkin 2005 review.
- I found no quantitative comparison of FC from different sensor types at one site, beyond the OSU qualitative statement that FC depends on sensor type.

## 3. Sensor time-series algorithms: detailed summary of the repo paper, plus related approaches

### Takeaway
Bean, Huffaker & Migliaccio (2018) show that automated processing of hourly θ time series can (i) find valid wetting–drying cycles and (ii) estimate FC within about 0.03 cm³/cm³ of an expert's manual values. Their tests used three Florida datasets on coarse, well-drained soils. SAX (trained) labeled cycles best. The training-free physics-based method (SWDP) and R `findpeaks` came close. For FC, `findpeaks` and exponential-decay regression (SWDP-R) agreed best with expert values. The key conceptual result is that FC from sensors is **temporally dynamic**: it varies from cycle to cycle with antecedent moisture. Later network-scale work (Oklahoma Mesonet; SMAP drydowns) applies similar "peak, then stabilization" logic at larger scales.

### Cited Findings: Bean, Huffaker & Migliaccio 2018 ([Bean2018](https://doi.org/10.2136/vzj2018.04.0073); local file `field_capacity_volumetric_water_content.md`)

**Citation and framing**
- Bean, E.Z., R.G. Huffaker, K.W. Migliaccio. 2018. "Estimating field capacity from volumetric soil water content time series using automated processing algorithms." *Vadose Zone J.* 17:180073, doi:10.2136/vzj2018.04.0073. It appeared in the UZIG special section, is open access (CC BY-NC-ND), and its data are on Dryad (doi:10.5061/dryad.f5220r3).
- Motivation: expert visual labeling of θ cycles is "arguably the most precise method" for FC but "impractically cumbersome" for large SMS datasets. The paper builds on Figueroa & Pope (2017, *Sensors* 17:1410), who found SAX-based labeling best across five SMS datasets and density-histogram labeling poor in four of five.
- Definition used: θFC is "the amount of water the soil can hold after gravity losses have occurred and before ET losses have started". Because ET is mostly diurnal, overnight θ decline is attributed mainly to drainage.

**Data (Table 1)**
- **CCS**: commercial citrus orchard with grower-managed irrigation, Fort Meade FL. Deep, well-drained, very rapidly permeable sands. Sensor at 15.2 cm (Campbell Scientific "SC161"), hourly, 1 Jul–2 Dec 2016.
- **TCS**: turfgrass (St. Augustine grass) with conventional irrigation scheduling, Homestead FL. Krome very gravelly loam (loamy-skeletal, carbonatic, hyperthermic Lithic Udorthent). Decagon ECH2O EC-5 at 7.6 cm, hourly, 20 Nov 2013–16 Nov 2014.
- **TET**: same site and sensors as TCS, but irrigation by an ET controller, so less water and lower θ than TCS.
- Reference ("true") values: manual expert labeling by K.W. Migliaccio. For each cycle, θA (wetting start), θB (peak) and θC (end of drying) were identified. A cycle was valid if (i) the rise matched a rain/irrigation event with no further input between 22:00 and the next scheduled irrigation (06:00/07:00), and (ii) θ declined steadily, with the nighttime drainage slope near zero. θFC = θ after one overnight drainage, i.e., the value just before scheduled irrigation.

**Cycle-labeling algorithms**
1. **SWDP (soil water dynamic principles, MATLAB)**: peaks θB are local maxima exceeding both neighbors by α = 0.006 (θi > θi±1 + α). θA is the preceding local minimum, which is also θC of the previous cycle. Cycles must pass a *peak-wetting* criterion (minimum rise, to reject noise) and a *drying-duration* criterion (enough drying to analyze FC). No training data needed.
2. **R-FP**: `pracma::findpeaks` returns the peak, the start of the rise and the end of the descent ("typically… the first plateau in the declining data"). Same wetting and duration screens. No training.
3. **R-SAX**: `jmotif`. Symbolic Aggregate approXimation: z-normalize, split into w segments (PAA means), map each to letters with equiprobable N(0,1) breakpoints (e.g., ±0.43 for a 3-letter alphabet), build sliding-window "wordbags", weight with tf·idf = log(1+tf)·log(N/N_word), and classify by cosine similarity to the valid training class. Uses expert labels with rotating cross-validation.
4. **R-DH**: `overlapping::overlap`. Mean overlap area of a test cycle's density histogram with the valid training cycles' histograms, compared with a critical threshold ("crit. dh", tested over several values; reported 0.06, 0.05, 0.01). Trained.

**FC estimators (applied to true-positive cycles from SWDP and R-FP)**
- **SWDP-K ("knee")**: θ where dθ/dt drops sharply, i.e., the first knee of the drying curve before morning ET.
- **SWDP-R (regression)**: fits overnight data (23:00–06:00) to exponential decay toward FC, θi = (θB − θFC)·e^(−λ·ti) + θFC, following Hillel (1998). The drainage rate is proportional to the excess over FC. θ is regressed on inverse time since peak (1/ti), and the intercept estimates θ at t → ∞ = θFC. FC is thus an *extrapolated* value, not an observed one.
- **R-FP plateau**: the end-of-descent point from `findpeaks`, described as a "quick and dirty" estimate.

**Results: cycle identification (accuracy / precision vs expert; Tables 2–4)**

| SWDP | 0.571 / 0.610 | 0.757 / 0.791 | 0.808 / 0.818 |
| R-FP | 0.671 / 0.786 | 0.549 / 0.678 | 0.533 / 0.559 |
| R-SAX | 0.681 / 0.833 | 0.768 / 0.800 | 0.899 / 0.857 |

- Ranking: R-SAX best (accuracy 0.681–0.899, precision 0.80–0.857, false positives 4–6, FP rate 0.143–0.20), then SWDP (accuracy 0.571–0.808, precision 0.61–0.818, FP 16–23, FP rate 0.182–0.390), then R-FP, then R-DH. SAX's advantage is attributed to training sets. SWDP and R-FP need no training or preprocessing and give "similar results".

**Results: FC estimation**
- Overall, all three automated estimators tracked the expert values' temporal fluctuations and "were typically within **0.03** of the true value" (cm³/cm³).
- **Systematic biases**: SWDP-K and R-FP generally *overestimated* FC. SWDP-R generally *underestimated* it, and gave the narrowest, most consistent range because it extrapolates rather than reading an observed value.
- **CCS (sand)**: true θFC 0.073–0.096 (mostly 0.08–0.09). Regression on the same cycles gave R² = 0.24 (R-FP), 0.48 (SWDP-R, smallest slope) and 0.90 (SWDP-K). Distribution (CDF) matching: R-FP best (R² = 0.95). SWDP-R CDF slope 0.55, i.e., about half the spread of the true values. CDF slopes overall 0.55–1.7, intercepts −0.053 to 0.032.
- **TCS (gravelly loam, wetter)**: true θFC ranged widely, **0.139–0.475**. Interquartile boxes of all methods lay within 0.390–0.451. Same-cycle regressions: R² > 0.97, slopes 0.98–1.02, intercepts −0.009 to 0.012, the best agreement of the three datasets. CDF slopes 0.84–0.97, R² 0.98–0.99. The automated methods missed some low true values (twice in April, once in September), overestimating FC.
- **TET**: medians of all methods 0.256–0.262. Same-cycle R² 0.89–0.97, slopes 0.95–0.96, intercepts 0.007–0.020. CDF slopes 0.97–1.00, R² 0.98 to >0.99. Good agreement within 0.22–0.29, with SWDP-K producing the five highest estimates.
- **Dynamic FC**: FC varied between cycles. For TCS/TET, increases between wetting events were usually <2.0% per day (maximum single-day +6.2%) and decreases usually <1.5% per day (maximum −6.8% over 2 days). FC was more stable before May and more dynamic afterward, which the authors attribute to changes in rainfall and possibly ET. They interpret this as antecedent-moisture and hysteresis effects (preferential flow in drier soil lowers retention; Merdun & Demirkiran 2008; Hardie et al. 2011). They argue MAD-based irrigation helps avoid low antecedent θ and therefore a reduced "functional" FC.

**Stated limitations**
- Only two locations, both relatively coarse-textured and well drained. Finer soils (clay, silt) or shallow confining layers "may produce less promising results".
- Upflux or capillary rise from shallow water tables was not considered.
- The "true" FC is itself an expert, convention-based value (one-night drainage), not an independent physical measurement. Single sensors at shallow depths (7.6 and 15.2 cm).
- The authors say the approaches "should be used with caution until they have been validated across a broader set of conditions".

### Cited Findings: related time-series and network approaches
- **Oklahoma Mesonet (Krueger & Ochsner 2024).** An automated routine found soil moisture peaks and the following "stable period" (FC), 1–10 days after the peak depending on texture. It was applied to sensors at 2, 10 and 24 in (5, 25, 60 cm) at **118 Mesonet stations, 2002–2022**, giving FC per site and depth. The Mesonet also has a lab database (Meso-Soil) of measured θ at −33 kPa for 117 stations at five depths, which could serve for lab-vs-sensor comparison — [OSU Extension fact sheet](https://extension.okstate.edu/fact-sheets/determining-field-capacity-using-continuous-soil-water-content-data.html); [Meso-Soil read-me](https://agriculture.okstate.edu/departments-programs/plant-soil/soil-physics/site-files/docs/meso-soil/read-me-a.pdf) (snippets).
- **Inference of hydrologic parameters from sensor records.** Chandler, Seyfried, McNamara & Hwang (2017, Front. Earth Sci. 5:25) infer soil hydrologic parameters (FC, onset of stress, extraction limits) from electronic θ records — cited in [Bean2018](https://doi.org/10.2136/vzj2018.04.0073). A search snippet linked to this article reported that FC estimates from **nine approaches had a coefficient of variation of 7–54%** — [Frontiers epub link from search](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/earth-science/articles/10.3389/feart.2017.00025/epub) (snippet; attribution to this paper should be confirmed).
- **Earlier time-series FC/texture work.** Ladson et al. (2006, Aust. J. Soil Res.) estimated extractable soil water for Australian soils from field θ ranges. Yang et al. (2016, Pedosphere) mapped texture from high-frequency field θ. Both are cited in [Bean2018](https://doi.org/10.2136/vzj2018.04.0073).
- **Site-specific FC and irrigation thresholds.** Kumar et al. (2023, Agric. Water Manage. 286:108385, "A methodology to optimize site-specific field capacity and irrigation thresholds") — cited in [Liu2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/agronomy15081973). Content not retrieved.
- **Drainage threshold from a mountain network.** Using a large mountainous soil moisture network (2014–2020), the θ threshold for drainage initiation "proves to be significantly lower than the commonly employed field capacity metric in hydrological models" — [DOAJ, "Drainage estimation across mountainous regions from large-scale soil moisture observations"](https://doaj.org/article/014871c157e241738ed49c75cddf83bf) (snippet).
- **Patent and industry logic.** θ "falls fairly quickly initially to a point where field capacity is reached, after which levels decrease at a much slower rate reflecting evapotranspiration". This is the basis of automatic irrigation threshold setting — [US Patent 8751052, "Automatic efficient irrigation threshold setting"](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8751052) (snippet).

### Inferences
- The repo paper's three estimators bracket the expert value: knee and plateau estimates are high, decay-regression estimates are low. A practical option is to report both, or to calibrate a bias correction per soil.
- A drainage-curve inflection (knee) and a plateau detector are the same "slope break" idea. The OSU/Mesonet "stable period" detector and the patent logic are versions of it at daily resolution. Frequency/histogram methods (R-DH in Bean2018) were the weakest labelers, and Figueroa & Pope (2017) also found them poor. Note that R-DH was used to *label cycles*, not to estimate FC directly from the θ histogram.
- The Bean et al. accuracy of about 0.03 cm³/cm³ is relative to an *expert convention*, not to an independent physical FC. It measures reproducibility of the convention, not absolute accuracy.

### Gaps
- **Histogram/frequency FC estimation.** This takes FC as an upper mode or high percentile of the θ probability distribution (e.g., the 90th–95th percentile of long records, or the "upper inflection" of the θ pdf as in stochastic soil-moisture models). I found no retrievable quantitative study comparing it with lab or field FC in this session.
- **SCAN, ISMN and COSMOS.** I found no retrievable study that derives FC network-wide from SCAN or ISMN with reported errors. UNVERIFIED leads to check: Dorigo et al. (ISMN, HESS 2011/2021). COSMOS cosmic-ray neutron sensing (Zreda et al. 2012, HESS) gives hectometre-scale θ, useful for footprint-scale FC; I found no COSMOS-specific FC accuracy figures.
- I did not access the Krueger & Ochsner (2024) primary paper's statistics comparing sensor FC with Meso-Soil θ(−33 kPa).

## 4. Pedotransfer functions (Rawls & Brakensiek, Saxton & Rawls 2006, Rosetta/Rosetta3, Wösten/HYPRES, Tóth et al. 2015, Van Looy et al. 2017, ML PTFs) and global maps (SoilGrids hydraulic layers, HiHydroSoil): typical accuracy

### Takeaway
PTFs estimate θ at −10/−33 kPa (or van Genuchten parameters) from texture, organic matter (OM)/organic carbon (OC) and bulk density. Typical water-retention RMSE is about **0.04–0.08 cm³/cm³**. Rosetta's water-retention RMSE fell from 0.078 (texture class only) to 0.044 cm³/cm³ (texture + bulk density + θ33 + θ1500). Independent tests of the Tóth et al. 2015 EU-PTFs found FC RMSE ≈ 0.033 cm³/cm³ (Croatia). PTF error is mostly a matter of input availability and transferability between regions. A PTF only reproduces its training definition of FC (θ at a fixed suction), so it inherits the −33 vs −10 kPa bias from Section 1. Global map products (HiHydroSoil, SoilGrids-derived) chain map uncertainty onto PTF uncertainty. I found no published accuracy statistics for HiHydroSoil.

### Cited Findings
- **Rosetta (Schaap, Leij & van Genuchten 2001, J. Hydrol. 251:163–176).** Five hierarchical neural-network PTFs with bootstrap uncertainty. Water-retention RMSE **decreased from 0.078 to 0.044 cm³/cm³** as predictors were added (textural class, then SSC, then +BD, then +θ33, then +θ1500) — [ResearchGate abstract](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/222687859_ROSETTA_A_computer_program_for_estimating_soil_hydraulic_parameters_with_hierarchical_pedotransfer_functions); [ISMC model portal, Rosetta](https://soil-modeling.org/resources-links/model-portal/rosetta) (abstract).
- **Rosetta3 (Zhang & Schaap 2017, J. Hydrol. 547:39–53).** Weighted recalibration "reduced modestly" the water-content RMSE compared with Rosetta1, while reducing matric-potential-dependent bias. It also gives improved parameter distributions and summary statistics — [ADS abstract](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017JHyd..547...39Z/abstract); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022169417300057) (abstract).
- **Rosetta transferred to Danish soils.** RMSE of **0.055 m³/m³** at the wet end, worse than a locally trained neural network — [ResearchGate, "Point and parameter pedotransfer functions for water retention predictions for Danish soils"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/223841561_Point_and_parameter_pedotransfer_functions_for_water_retention_predictions_for_Danish_soils) (snippet).
- **Saxton & Rawls (2006, SSSAJ 70:1569–1578; doi:10.2136/sssaj2005.0117).** New soil water characteristic equations from the USDA soil database using only texture and OM. They were verified against independent datasets over a wide range of textures and implemented in a graphical model (SPAW) — [Center for Watershed Protection abstract page](https://owl.cwp.org/?p=11829); [CiteDrive record](https://citedrive.com/en/discovery/soil-water-characteristic-estimates-by-texture-and-organic-matter-for-hydrologic-solutions). The equations are embedded in USDA-ARS WEPP code, where θ(33 kPa) is labeled "field capacity" and θ(1500 kPa) "wilting point" — [WEPP source, saxpar.for](https://infosys.ars.usda.gov/svn/code/weps1/branches/weps.src.wepp/src/lib_wepp/saxpar.for) (snippet). Saxton–Rawls PTFs are also used in Earth-system and land-model parameterization — [GMD 2016-165 discussion](https://gmd.copernicus.org/preprints/gmd-2016-165/gmd-2016-165.pdf) (snippet).
- **Tóth et al. 2015 (Eur. J. Soil Sci. 66:226–238; doi:10.1111/ejss.12192), the EU-PTFs ("euptfv1").** Built on **EU-HYDI**, the most comprehensive European hydro-pedological database. Predicts θ at several matric potentials and VG/MvG parameters using linear regression (quantitative inputs), regression trees (mixed inputs) and class means (qualitative inputs only) — [ESDAC/JRC page](https://esdac.jrc.ec.europa.eu/node/67252); [PMC4386477](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4386477/) (abstract).
- **Independent validation of Tóth 2015 (Croatia).** FC RMSE **3.3 vol% (≈0.033 cm³/cm³)** and wilting point RMSE 4.9 vol%, with wilting point overestimated — [CROSBI record](https://www.bib.irb.hr/1215156) (snippet).
- **euptfv2 (Szabó, Weynants & Weber 2021, GMD 14:151).** Random-forest EU-PTFs with prediction uncertainty. They predict θ at −100 cm (−10 kPa) and −330 cm (−33 kPa) and are "significantly more accurate than euptfv1" — [GMD 14:151, 2021](https://gmd.copernicus.org/articles/14/151/2021/); [JRC repository](https://publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu/repository/handle/JRC123775) (abstract).
- **Van Looy et al. 2017 (Rev. Geophys. 55:1199–1256; doi:10.1002/2017RG000581).** Reviews existing PTFs and documents a "new generation" across Earth-system disciplines. It argues that PTF development must be paired with extrapolation/upscaling techniques to capture spatial variability for ESMs. Open-access PDF — [Univ. Reading repository](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/73853); [PDF](https://reading-9.eprints-hosting.org/73853/1/Looy_et_al-2017-Reviews_of_Geophysics.pdf); [Eos Editors' Vox](https://eos.org/editors-vox/pedotransfer-functions-bring-new-life-to-earth-system-modeling) (abstract).
- **ML PTFs, random forest vs regression (Iran, 223 samples).** With inputs sand, clay, BD and measured θFC/θPWP, the best RF and nonlinear-regression PTFs reached integral RMSE (IRMSE) of **0.039 and 0.032 cm³/cm³** for the whole retention curve. RF was *less reliable* than nonlinear regression, with overprediction and test instability — [J. Areeo article 100011](https://journals.areeo.ac.ir/article_100011.html) (abstract). In another Iranian study, Rosetta's NN had the lowest error (RMSE 0.031) for the water characteristic curve — same search set (snippet; units/source uncertain).
- **ML vs Rosetta for Ks (Czech, 126 datasets).** Random Forest beat boosted regression trees, but Rosetta's NN PTFs ranked in the top four of five — [Plant Soil Environ. 2022, 68(7):338–346](https://pse.agriculturejournals.cz/artkey/pse-202207-0005_a-comparison-of-measured-and-estimated-saturated-hydraulic-conductivity-of-various-soils-in-the-czech-republic.php) (abstract). This is Ks, not FC, but it shows ML PTFs do not automatically outperform Rosetta.
- **Hybrid ANN for FC/PWP.** Vaheddoost, Guan & Mohammadi (2020, Environ. Sci. Pollut. Res. 27:13131–13141), "Application of hybrid ANN–whale optimization model in evaluation of the field capacity and the permanent wilting point of the soils" — cited in [Liu2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/agronomy15081973) (ref. 68). Metrics not retrieved.
- **Pachepsky/Nemes regression trees for FC directly.** Regression trees predicting *field-measured* FC rather than θ33 from soil properties (30 US locations) — [Pachepsky et al. 2008](https://scisoc.confex.com/crops/2008am/webprogram/Paper44656.html). Texture-specific bias "greatly reduced" — [Nemes et al. 2010](https://old.iuss.org/19th%20WCSS/Symposium/pdf/0021.pdf).
- **HiHydroSoil (FutureWater).** v1.2 (2016) was a 1 km product built on SoilGrids1km. v2.0 (Simons, Koster & Droogers 2020, FutureWater Report 213) is built on SoilGrids250m and applies PTFs to derive OM, texture class, Ks, MvG α and n, and θr/θs. FC can be derived from these parameters at −10/−33 kPa. The product pages give **no quantitative error metrics** — [HiHydroSoil v2.0 report](https://www.futurewater.nl/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/HiHydroSoil-v2.0-High-Resolution-Soil-Maps-of-Global-Hydraulic-Properties_v2.pdf); [v1 report](https://www.futurewater.nl/wp-content/uploads/2015/05/HiHydroSoil-A-high-resolution-soil-map-of-hydraulic-properties.pdf); [GEE community catalog](https://gee-community-catalog.org/projects/hihydro_soil/) (snippets).
- **Tropical caveat (brief).** A Brazil-specific θ6 PTF reached RMSR 0.026 m³/m³ for field FC, better than classical fixed-suction PTFs — [Embrapa 2016](https://www.alice.cnptia.embrapa.br/alice/bitstream/doc/1051668/1/2016026.pdf). Tropical specifics are covered by another researcher.

### Inferences
- A realistic accuracy hierarchy for FC-type θ (cm³/cm³), from the figures above:
  - Texture class only: about 0.08.
  - Texture + BD/OM: about 0.04–0.06.
  - With measured retention points as input: about 0.03–0.045.
  - Locally calibrated or field-FC-targeted PTFs: about 0.026–0.033.
- Most of the 0.08 cm³/cm³ θ33-vs-field-FC bias (Pachepsky et al.) is *definitional*. A PTF with a perfect θ33 prediction can still miss field FC by that much. For modeling, the choice of target variable (θ10, θ33 or a flux-based FC) can matter as much as the PTF algorithm.
- ML PTFs (RF, ANN, euptfv2) help mainly when training data are large and inputs are rich. On small regional datasets, RF can overfit (Iran study), and Rosetta stays competitive.

### Gaps
- **Saxton & Rawls (2006) accuracy (UNVERIFIED).** I recall the paper reporting about R² ≈ 0.63 and SE ≈ 0.05 cm³/cm³ for θ33, and R² ≈ 0.86, SE ≈ 0.02 for θ1500. I could not confirm these; check the paper's Table 2.
- **Rawls & Brakensiek (1982/1985) and Rawls et al. (1982, Trans. ASAE).** I retrieved no accuracy figures. UNVERIFIED: the Rawls et al. 1982 regression for θ33 is often reported at an RMSE of about 0.04–0.06 cm³/cm³.
- **Wösten et al. HYPRES (1999, Geoderma 90:169–185).** No metrics retrieved. UNVERIFIED: continuous HYPRES PTFs are commonly reported at an RMSE of about 0.04–0.05 cm³/cm³ over the retention curve.
- **Tóth et al. 2015 own validation RMSE** at −100/−330 cm: not retrieved (PMC blocked). The figure I recall, about 0.04–0.05 cm³/cm³ depending on inputs, is UNVERIFIED.
- **SoilGrids water-retention layers.** ISRIC released global water-retention maps at 10, 33 and 1500 kPa from WoSIS (UNVERIFIED reference: Turek et al. 2023, *Int. Soil Water Conserv. Res.*). Their accuracy statistics were not retrieved. Other global hydraulic datasets (Montzka et al. 2017 ESSD; Dai et al. 2019 ESSD; Zhang et al. 2018 global Rosetta-based maps) were not checked.
- **Van Looy et al. 2017** includes summary tables of PTF performance that I could not access. The open PDF linked above is the place to get them.

## 5. Inverse modeling, remote sensing and data assimilation approaches to FC (SMAP drydowns, McColl et al. 2017)

### Takeaway
Three groups of approaches exist:
- **Inverse modeling** (e.g., HYDRUS-1D) fits retention and conductivity parameters to in situ θ series, sometimes constrained by measured FC. Liu et al. (2025) got R² ≈ 0.81 and RMSE ≈ 0.018 cm³/cm³ in calibration.
- **Flux-based or analytical definitions** derive FC from hydraulic parameters: K(θ) falls to a negligible flux (Meyer & Gee 1999; Twarakavi et al. 2009), or the loss of hydraulic continuity (Assouline & Or 2014). Stochastic ecohydrology models define FC as the θ where K is 10% of maximum daily ET (Laio et al. 2001).
- **Satellite drydown analysis** (McColl et al. 2017 with SMAP) fits exponential decay to surface θ after rain. It gives drainage/drying timescales and a lower asymptote, not FC directly. It is limited to about 5 cm depth and coarse footprints.

### Cited Findings
- **HYDRUS-1D inverse modeling constrained by measured FC.** Liu et al. estimated soil hydraulic parameters by inverse modeling with HYDRUS-1D ("Water Flow–Soil Hydraulic Parameters"). The constraints were daily mean in situ θ, measured FC, particle-size distribution and bulk density, over 10 layers (0–100 cm), using three years (2021–2023) of tea-plantation and grassland data. Calibration gave R² = 0.806 (tea) and 0.809 (grassland), tea RMSE = 0.01824 and MAE = 0.0144 cm³/cm³. Validation R² was 0.739 (tea) and 0.878 (grassland) — [Liu2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/agronomy15081973) (local file). Literature benchmarks quoted there for HYDRUS: R² 0.74–0.93 calibration and 0.79–0.91 validation (Guo et al. 2024).
- **Inverse calibration from sensor data.** Ritter et al. (2003, Agric. Water Manage. 59:77–96) calibrated van Genuchten parameters in the WAVE model from field θ using two inverse methods, as an alternative to direct methods — cited in [Bean2018](https://doi.org/10.2136/vzj2018.04.0073).
- **Flux-based FC.** FC is the θ at which drainage flux ceases or becomes negligible; θ at about 1/3 bar ignores this flux basis — [Meyer & Gee 1999](https://ascelibrary.com/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%291090-0241%281999%29125%3A7%28595%29) (abstract). A modified numerical approach to flux-based FC is in [ASCE J. Hydrol. Eng., "Modified Numerical Approach to Estimate Field Capacity"](https://ascelibrary.com/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%29HE.1943-5584.0000662) (title only).
- **Twarakavi, Sakai & Šimůnek (2009), "An objective analysis of the dynamic nature of field capacity", WRR 45:W10410, doi:10.1029/2009WR007944.** Later authors describe it as a "parametric" evaluation generalized across many soils — [EarthRef record](https://earthref.org/ERR/125082); [HESSD 2011 discussion comment](https://hess.copernicus.org/preprints/8/C2835/2011/hessd-8-C2835-2011.pdf) (snippet).
- **Assouline & Or (2014, WRR), "The concept of field capacity revisited".** They use an intrinsic characteristic length derived from the drainable pore-size distribution to mark loss of hydraulic continuity, linked to reaching FC. The static criterion is extended to a dynamic criterion for internal drainage. FC is related to the van Genuchten n, air-entry value and θr through a capillary–gravity balance. They note FC "had been fraught with ambiguities and inconsistencies since its inception" — [AGU FM14 abstract](https://agu.confex.com/agu/fm14/webprogram/Paper11291.html); [EGU2015 abstract](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU2015/EGU2015-3156.pdf) (abstracts).
- **Ecohydrology-model definition.** "The field capacity s_fc… is operationally defined as the value of soil moisture at which the hydraulic conductivity becomes negligible (here 10%) compared to the maximum daily evapotranspiration losses E_max (… assumed to be 0.5 cm/d)", computed from the power-law K(s) — [Laio2001](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0309-1708(01)00005-7) (local file). The same definition, citing Rodríguez-Iturbe & Porporato (2004), appears in [Pelak2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.advwatres.2019.103411) (local file). Pelak & Porporato also model the pore-size distribution, and hence retention and FC, as *time-varying* under management and SOM change, unlike static PTFs.
- **McColl et al. 2017, "Global characterization of surface soil moisture drydowns", GRL, doi:10.1002/2017GL072819.** SMAP surface θ after rain was fit to an exponential decay. Drying timescales were shorter in more arid regions and in sandier soils, but the texture effect was "relatively minor". Much of the variance was unexplained, with vegetation a likely factor. A later critique notes that aridity index and texture are fixed in time — [HESS 22:1649, 2018 (follow-up citing McColl)](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/22/1649/2018/); [LSU repository, "Global Surface Soil Moisture Drydown Patterns"](https://repository.lsu.edu/plantsoil_pubs/699) (snippets).
- **SMAP-scale drying is water-limited.** Shellito et al. found surface drying at SMAP scales to be water-limited, consistent with McColl et al. (2017) — [SMAP JPL resource, Shellito](https://smap.jpl.nasa.gov/system/internal_resources/details/original/476_218_Shellito.pdf) (snippet).
- **Texture matters only when dry.** Sehgal, Gaur & Mohanty (2021) found texture influences retention and drydown parameters "only when the footprint mean θ is low, specifically in arid and sparsely vegetated regions" — [HESS 2017-338 review materials / follow-up](https://hess.copernicus.org/preprints/hess-2017-338/hess-2017-338-AR1.pdf) (snippet; venue attribution approximate).
- **Network drainage threshold below model FC.** A large-scale mountain network found the drainage-initiation θ threshold "significantly lower than the commonly employed field capacity metric in hydrological models" — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/014871c157e241738ed49c75cddf83bf) (snippet). This is direct empirical support for recalibrating model FC from data.

### Inferences
- Remote-sensing drydowns constrain *effective, footprint-scale drainage/drying behavior* of the top about 5 cm. They are better suited to calibrating land-surface-model drainage or FC parameters through data assimilation than to producing agronomic FC values for root zones.
- Flux-based definitions (Meyer & Gee; Twarakavi; Laio's K = 0.1·Emax) make FC model-consistent. A model's FC then follows from its own K(θ) and a chosen threshold flux. They also make FC dependent on that threshold, which is the same arbitrariness seen in field tests (Section 2).
- Bean2018's SWDP-R exponential decay toward θFC is the point-scale counterpart of the SMAP drydown fit. Both assume a linear-reservoir drainage term, so drydown parameters from sensors and satellites are conceptually comparable.

### Gaps
- **McColl et al. 2017 quantitative results (UNVERIFIED).** The model is θ(t) = Δθ·exp(−t/τ) + θ_w with a global median τ of a few days, and SMAP's 2–3 day revisit biases against short τ. I could not retrieve the GRL text to confirm numbers or whether an upper bound relates to FC.
- **Akbar et al. (2018, WRR), "Estimation of landscape soil water losses from satellite observations of soil moisture".** UNVERIFIED reference. It derives SMAP loss functions dL/dt(θ) with breakpoints interpretable as FC-like and stress thresholds. I could not retrieve it.
- **Twarakavi et al. (2009) closed form (UNVERIFIED).** An approximate closed form for FC from van Genuchten n and Ks (Se_fc ≈ n^(−0.60(2+log10 Ks)), Ks in cm/d) needs verification from the WRR paper.
- **Data assimilation.** I found no retrieved study that assimilates SMAP/ISMN data specifically to estimate FC with reported accuracy. Ensemble Kalman filter parameter-estimation studies in land-surface models (e.g., NASA Catchment, Noah-MP) are leads, not verified.

## 6. Which methods are recommended for which use (irrigation scheduling, models, mapping)?

### Takeaway
- **Irrigation scheduling at a site**: in situ, sensor-derived FC is preferred. Use automated time-series algorithms (Bean2018 SWDP-R/R-FP; Mesonet stable-period method) or NRCS-style visual review, ideally updated over time since FC is dynamic. Lab θ at −10/−33 kPa needs a local correction.
- **Process models**: use a definition consistent with the model's K(θ), either flux-based or inverse-calibrated against θ series (HYDRUS-1D constrained by measured FC).
- **Regional or global mapping**: use PTFs (Rosetta3, euptfv2, Saxton–Rawls) on gridded soil data (SoilGrids → HiHydroSoil), accepting RMSE of about 0.03–0.08 cm³/cm³ plus definitional bias, and quantify uncertainty.

### Cited Findings
- **Irrigation.** Efficient irrigation keeps θ between θFC and a minimum acceptable θ (MAD) to limit drainage loss. Accurate θFC from valid cycles "is critical to irrigation efficiency and water conservation". Automated approaches give "objective, repeatable, and efficient processes" vs subjective manual determination, and there is "a need for automated, objective analysis to leverage [FC] dynamics in irrigation management and modeling" — [Bean2018](https://doi.org/10.2136/vzj2018.04.0073).
- **Automatic threshold setting.** Smart-irrigation controllers set thresholds from the post-irrigation slope change ("FC reached, then slower ET-driven decline") — [US Patent 8751052](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8751052) (snippet). Kumar et al. 2023 (AWM) propose optimizing site-specific FC and irrigation thresholds — cited in [Liu2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/agronomy15081973).
- **Producers with sensors.** OSU Extension recommends continuous-sensor FC determination because standard methods are lacking and lab measurements are hard to do accurately, with the caveat that results depend on sensor type — [OSU Extension fact sheet](https://extension.okstate.edu/fact-sheets/determining-field-capacity-using-continuous-soil-water-content-data.html).
- **Fine-textured soils, conservative thresholds.** Pressure-plate FC was judged more suitable for fine-textured soils when a conservative threshold is wanted — [Irrig. Sci. 2025](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s00271-025-01058-1) (snippet).
- **Models.** Use measured FC as a constraint on inverse estimation of hydraulic parameters for physically based models (HYDRUS-1D) — [Liu2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/agronomy15081973). Stochastic water-balance models use a K-based FC tied to ET — [Laio2001](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0309-1708(01)00005-7). FC thresholds in hydrological models may be set too high relative to observed drainage-initiation thresholds — [DOAJ mountain network study](https://doaj.org/article/014871c157e241738ed49c75cddf83bf).
- **Mapping and ESMs.** PTFs paired with upscaling/extrapolation are the route for ESM parameterization — [Van Looy et al. 2017](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/73853). HiHydroSoil v2.0 provides 250 m PTF-derived hydraulic parameters from SoilGrids — [FutureWater](https://www.futurewater.nl/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/HiHydroSoil-v2.0-High-Resolution-Soil-Maps-of-Global-Hydraulic-Properties_v2.pdf). euptfv2 provides uncertainty-aware European predictions — [GMD 2021](https://gmd.copernicus.org/articles/14/151/2021/).
- **Disagreement among methods.** FC estimates from nine approaches showed CVs of 7–54% — [snippet linked to Frontiers 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/earth-science/articles/10.3389/feart.2017.00025/epub) (attribution to confirm).

### Inferences
Comparison matrix, synthesized from the sections above:

| Method | Data needs | Typical accuracy / bias | Best use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lab θ at −33 kPa (pressure plate) | Cores, lab days–weeks | Biased vs field FC (θ33 ≈ 0.08 below field FC, US data); fine soils show plate errors (mainly dry end) | Standardized databases, PTF training; US convention |
| Lab θ at −10/−6 kPa (sandbox, tension table, plate) | Undisturbed cores | Closer to field FC in many soils (θ6 best in Brazil) | Agronomic FC where field data are absent |
| Field ponding / internal drainage | Plot, θ and ψ sensors, 2–10+ days, labor | Reference by definition, but threshold- and time-dependent; layering effects | Benchmark and calibration of other methods |
| Sensor time-series algorithms | ≥1 season of hourly or daily θ, rain/irrigation log | Within ~0.03 of expert convention (coarse soils); dynamic FC; sensor-type dependent | Irrigation scheduling, site models |
| PTFs (Rosetta3, Saxton–Rawls, EU-PTF, ML) | Texture ± OM, BD, θ33/θ1500 | RMSE ~0.03–0.08 cm³/cm³; inherits training-suction bias | Regional models, data-poor sites |
| Global maps (SoilGrids → HiHydroSoil) | None (download) | Map error + PTF error; no published FC accuracy found | Continental/global LSM/ESM, screening |
| Inverse modeling (HYDRUS) | θ series, forcing, soil data | Model fit RMSE ~0.02 cm³/cm³ (fit, not FC error); non-uniqueness | Process models needing consistent K(θ) and FC |
| Satellite drydowns (SMAP) | Public data | Surface ~5 cm, ~9–36 km scale; timescales, not FC | LSM drainage parameter calibration, DA |

- For a literature review: FC is best treated as a **definition plus method** pair (suction-based, flux-threshold-based, time-after-wetting-based or algorithm-based). Accuracy figures are only comparable within the same definition.

### Gaps
- I found no authoritative guideline (e.g., FAO-56, NRCS) retrieved in this session that ranks FC methods by use case. The recommendations above are synthesized from the cited studies.
- No retrieved study directly compared, at one site, lab FC, field-drainage FC, sensor-algorithm FC and PTF FC together with errors. Such a four-way comparison would be the most useful evidence and appears to be missing or not found.
