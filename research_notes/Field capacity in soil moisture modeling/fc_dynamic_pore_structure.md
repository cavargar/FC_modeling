## 1. How tillage, post-tillage consolidation, rainfall slaking and compaction alter the PSD and the water content at −10/−33 kPa

### Takeaway
Tillage creates a large, unstable interaggregate (structural) pore fraction. Wetting–drying (W/D) cycles, rain impact and traffic then destroy it within weeks to months, and sometimes within 1–4 W/D cycles. The changes are concentrated in the wet range of the retention curve, roughly 0 to −33 kPa. As a result, θ at −10 kPa (and θs) is strongly time-dependent, while θ at −33 kPa changes less and can even rise per unit bulk volume as the soil densifies. Near-saturated K is the most sensitive property (up to 100-fold declines). The field literature is inconsistent on the net tillage effect, because the time since disturbance often outweighs the treatment.

### Cited Findings

**Mechanisms and the "structural range" (primary repo source)**
- Or et al. treat two processes as controlling post-tillage evolution: (1) aggregate disintegration during W/D, which occurs mainly while water infiltrates relatively dry soil and "practically ceases" once the soil is uniformly wetted, so it is better modeled as discrete events; and (2) slow viscoplastic deformation and rejoining (coalescence) of wet aggregates, driven by negative pore-water pressure at menisci between aggregates — [or2000.md](/home/user/cavargar/fc_modeling/or2000.md)
- Working hypothesis of Or et al.: the structural (interaggregate) pore space "corresponding roughly to the 0–33 kPa soil water retention range" is the fraction most affected by tillage. Textural (intra-aggregate) pore space is treated as relatively stable. Posttillage changes therefore concern primarily the "effective pore space" within 0–33 kPa (citing Ahuja et al. 1998) — [or2000.md](/home/user/cavargar/fc_modeling/or2000.md)
- Or et al. state that the observed posttillage evolution pathways are: loss of large pores (a shift to smaller mean pore size), reduction in overall porosity, and a possible change in PSD variance. Larger pores deform faster than smaller ones (Scherer 1984; Ghezzehei & Or 2000) — [or2000.md](/home/user/cavargar/fc_modeling/or2000.md)
- The effect of compaction (coalescence) "is reflected mostly in the wet range (0 > ψ > −2 m)", i.e. 0 to about −20 kPa (citing Mapa et al. 1986; Silva 1995; Ahuja et al. 1998) — [or2000.md](/home/user/cavargar/fc_modeling/or2000.md)
- Or et al. report a fast timescale: in aggregated, expansive Millville silt loam columns, three W/D cycles (30 min sprinkling followed by 30 min drainage) changed the soil water characteristic within a few hours (Silva 1995). Total porosity declined over about 18,000 s, faster for 2–4 mm aggregates than for the "natural" <8 mm distribution, so the initial aggregate size controls the rate — [or2000.md](/home/user/cavargar/fc_modeling/or2000.md)
- Or et al. also report a slow timescale: in less expansive Cotto clay (Puerto Rico), changes in the soil water characteristic and in relative interaggregate porosity (tilled/no-till porosity, ranging about 0.04–0.18) were tracked over more than 50 weeks after tillage (Rivadeneira 1982). Mapa et al. (1986) report similar rates for two Hawaiian soils — [or2000.md](/home/user/cavargar/fc_modeling/or2000.md)
- Or et al. note that similar porosity or bulk density can give very different retention and intake properties (Nimmo & Akstin 1988), so the whole PSD, not just bulk density, must be tracked — [or2000.md](/home/user/cavargar/fc_modeling/or2000.md)
- In Or et al.'s unit-cell geometry (cubic packing of 50 µm aggregates), only 5% axial strain reduces the central pore radius from 48.5 to 43.0 µm and the porosity from 0.48 to 0.39 — [or2000.md](/home/user/cavargar/fc_modeling/or2000.md)

**Field and experimental magnitudes**
- Ahuja et al. (1998), SSSAJ 62:1228–1233, reviewed literature and data. Under field conditions tillage did not significantly change the air-entry value, but it steepened the retention curve below air entry. The changes occurred "only in the larger pore-size range, approximately between the air-entry pressure head value and 10 times the air-entry value." The authors proposed estimating the tilled-soil curve from the untilled one, either by imposing a Brooks–Corey segment over that range or by assuming Δθ ∝ 1/h, and validated this on 4 curve pairs from 3 soils (abstract) — [AGRIS record](https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/65df55264c5aef494fe1cf61)
- Mapa, Green & Santo (1986), SSSAJ 50:1133–1138, worked on two Hawaiian soils (Molokai silty clay loam, a Typic Torrox; Waialua clay loam) with traffic and raindrop impact excluded, so the changes were driven only by effective-stress changes during W/D. Near-saturated K fell 100-fold with W/D after tillage. Water retention changed noticeably after only one W/D cycle, and sorptivity reached an asymptote after about 2 cycles (abstract) — [ADS abstract](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986SSASJ..50.1133M/abstract)
- Zhang, Lu, Heitman, Horton & Ren (2017), SSSAJ 81(6):1288–1295, studied a 0–15 cm tilled layer. Total porosity decreased with time and then stabilized after 4 W/D cycles. The retention curves shifted toward higher water content at a given matric potential. The change was small in W/D cycles 1–3, when saturation was low, peaked after the 4th cycle, when the soil was nearly saturated, and was less significant thereafter. The authors conclude that the "θ–ψm relationships following tillage are dynamic" (abstract) — [Wiley](https://acsess.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.2136/sssaj2017.01.0038); [AGRIS](https://agris.fao.org/search/en/providers/122535/records/65dfc4eeb766d82b180315f6)
- Schwen, Bodner, Scholl, Buchan & Loiskandl (2011), *Soil & Tillage Research* 113(2):89–98, compared conventional, reduced and no-till. Citing papers report that macropore flow was affected more by measurement time than by tillage type (secondary description) — [T&F citing paper](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/00288233.2019.1684323)
- Strudley, Green & Ascough (2008), *Soil & Tillage Research* 99(1):4–48, doi:10.1016/j.still.2008.01.007, is a review of tillage and residue effects on the spatial and temporal variability of soil hydraulic properties, including compaction, topography and modeling — [USDA-ARS record](https://www.ars.usda.gov/research/publications/publication/?seqNo115=207371); [ScienceDirect](https://sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167198708000196). According to citing works (secondary), the review found no consistent tillage-system effect on soil hydraulic properties. Ks and infiltration results were inconsistent across textures and climates, and effects that are pronounced right after tillage "can diminish rapidly" — [ResearchGate entry](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/222690667_Tillage_effects_on_soil_hydraulic_properties_in_space_and_time_State_of_the_science)
- An INRAE paper by Alletto and colleagues cites Alletto & Coquet (2009) for the view that tillage is a major source of spatial and temporal variability in surface-horizon properties, and states that tillage-created porosity disappears fairly quickly through natural reconsolidation by W/D cycles (Ahuja et al. 1998) (secondary) — search snippets of [hal.inrae.fr/hal-03853888](https://hal.inrae.fr/hal-03853888/document) and [hal-03206553](https://hal.inrae.fr/hal-03206553v1/document)
- Hu et al. (2009), *Geoderma* 149:355–366 (Loess Plateau Entisol), used disc infiltrometry. Ks and the Gardner α were influenced more by time than by land use. Over time, the flow contributions of macropores and mesopores decreased and that of micropores increased (abstract) — [NWAFU repository](https://sbir.nwafu.edu.cn/handle/361005/4819)
- Pirlot, Renard, De Clerck & Degré (2024), *European Journal of Soil Science*, ran a 3-year field study (2021 wet, 2022 severe drought, 2023 intermediate) with field-measured retention curves and bimonthly bulk density. Retention curves "varied considerably over time and between systems", which the authors link to crop type, weed control, residue management, harvest compaction and temporary grassland. They note that such dynamics "are rarely taken into account in models" (abstract) — [ORBi PDF](https://orbi.uliege.be/bitstream/2268/321866/1/European%20J%20Soil%20Science%20-%202024%20-%20Pirlot%20-%20How%20does%20soil%20water%20retention%20change%20over%20time%20%20A%20three%e2%80%90year%20field%20study%20under.pdf)
- Jirků et al. (2013), *Geoderma* 204–205:43–58, sampled three topsoils (Haplic Cambisol, Greyic Phaeozem, Haplic Luvisol) monthly for retention and K over 2007–2010. Seasonal and annual variability was documented, with the first year more variable than the second (abstract or record level; no retention numbers found) — [ASEP record](https://asep.lib.cas.cz/arl-cav/cs/detail-cav_un_epca-0394222-Temporal-variability-of-structure-and-hydraulic-properties-of-topsoil-of-three-soil-types)

**Rainfall slaking and sealing**
- Assouline (2004), "Rainfall-induced soil surface sealing: a critical review…", *Vadose Zone Journal* 3:570–591, is the standard review; only the citation was confirmed. One study citing it reports that unsaturated K decreased by about 60% on average within 2–9 min of rain as the seal formed, attributed to lower seal porosity and higher "ineffective" water content (snippet) — [IJSWR article](https://ijswr.ut.ac.ir/article_63426.html?lang=en)
- Or et al. distinguish the surface crust (raindrop impact, ponding-induced slaking; a thin layer) from the subsurface plough layer. In the plough layer wetting is more gradual and under tension, so structural change is more gradual — [or2000.md](/home/user/cavargar/fc_modeling/or2000.md)
- Chandrasekhar et al. (2019, *Geoderma*) tested the Leij–Or pore-evolution model on global retention datasets. Agreement was weaker for pores ≤10 µm radius, "especially under intense rainfall" — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/334536437_Modeling_the_evolution_of_soil_structural_pore_space_in_agricultural_soils_following_tillage); [TU Dresden record](https://fis.tu-dresden.de/portal/en/publications/modeling-the-evolution-of-soil-structural-pore-space-in-agricultural-soils-following-tillage(b14e3fee-fa94-4423-bf45-53eec32a60cf).html)

**Compaction**
- Obour & Ugarte (2021), *Soil & Tillage Research* 211:105019, meta-analysed 51 studies of axle load, wheel passes and tyre pressure. Compaction raised penetration resistance by 99%, 94% and 41% in medium-, coarse- and fine-textured soils. The outcome set includes water-filled pore volume and air permeability at FC and Ks. The θFC effect size was not retrievable — [Illinois Experts](https://experts.illinois.edu/en/publications/a-meta-analysis-of-the-impact-of-traffic-induced-compaction-on-so/); [UG Space](https://ugspace.ug.edu.gh/items/3439b8ec-fb5d-487e-911e-228c25febc41)
- In lab retention curves from German farmland, the van Genuchten α often decreased after traffic, i.e. the dominant pore size shifted finer (EGU23 abstract) — [EGU23-11665](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU23/EGU23-11665.html)
- In one traffic-load study, compacted treatments held more water than controls at drier potentials (at −25 cm H2O, about −2.5 kPa) (snippet) — [CiteDrive / SSSAJ 10.1002/saj2.70281](https://www.citedrive.com/en/discovery/response-of-selected-soil-physical-properties-to-various-traffic-loads-at-three-field-landscape-positions/)
- Bravo Peña et al. (2025), *Vadose Zone Journal*, fitted unimodal and bimodal (dual) van Genuchten models to dynamic in-situ retention in a compacted Andosol at several compaction levels, separating W/D phases with dθ/dt — [Wiley VZJ](https://acsess.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/vzj2.70021)

### Inferences
- **The FC definition matters.** In Or et al. and Ahuja et al., the tillage-sensitive range is 0 to about 10× air-entry suction. For a structural air entry of about −1 to −3 kPa, that range ends at about −10 to −30 kPa. So θ at −10 kPa (common for coarse soils and "in-situ FC") should be far more time-variable than θ at −33 kPa. Capillary radii make the same point: r ≈ 2γ/|ψ| gives about 14 µm at −10 kPa and 4.4 µm at −33 kPa, while the structural pore median in Or et al. is about 48 µm (air entry about −3 kPa). This is a calculation from the cited physics, not a measured result.
- **Direction of the FC change.** Consolidation removes large pores and shrinks the bulk volume. θs and macroporosity drop, but volumetric θ at −33 kPa can stay constant or increase, as Zhang et al. 2017 observed, because textural pores occupy a larger share of a smaller volume (the textural porosity in Or et al.'s Table 2 rises from 0.30 to 0.33). A constant-FC model that uses a fresh-tillage curve would therefore likely overestimate drainable porosity and underestimate post-consolidation volumetric FC.
- **Event-driven timing.** Change is event-driven and depends on saturation: Zhang et al. saw the largest change in the cycle that reached near-saturation. This suggests that the clock for FC evolution should be W/D or rainfall events, not calendar time.

### Gaps
- No full-text quantitative θ(−10 kPa) and θ(−33 kPa) time series were retrievable for Schwen et al. 2011, Strudley et al. 2008, Alletto & Coquet 2009, Zhang et al. 2017 or Jirků et al. 2013, because downloads were blocked. Typical Δθ magnitudes at FC potentials (e.g. 0.01–0.05 m³ m⁻³) could therefore not be sourced and should not be stated without checking the full texts.
- The "Alletto et al. 2010 Geoderma" paper could not be located; Alletto & Coquet 2009 is the commonly cited work. Schwen et al. 2011 exists in two papers (*Soil & Tillage Research* 113:89–98 and *Agricultural Water Management* 99:42–50). No *Vadose Zone Journal* paper was found.
- No compaction meta-analysis effect size for θFC was retrieved.

## 2. Effects of soil organic carbon and amendments on FC and available water capacity (AWC)

### Takeaway
SOC raises θ at FC more than θ at the wilting point, but the net AWC gain is small. The best-supported global estimate is about +1.16 mm per 100 mm of soil per +10 g C kg⁻¹, with FC +1.61 mm/100 mm, largest in sands and smallest in clays. Biochar has a large effect on FC in coarse soils (+51%) and almost none in fine soils. The PP2019 model embeds SOM as a slow, continuous driver of all three PSD parameters. Because SOC itself changes over years to decades, FC drifts over the same timescales.

### Cited Findings
- Minasny & McBratney (2018), "Limited effect of organic matter on soil available water capacity", combined a meta-analysis of 60 studies with databases of more than 50,000 measurements. A 1% mass increase in OC (10 g C kg⁻¹) raises θ at saturation by 2.95, FC by 1.61, WP by 0.17 and AWC by 1.16 mm H2O per 100 mm soil. 75% of studies report 0.7–2 mm/100 mm. The effect is largest in sands, then loams, and least in clays. The authors call the AWC benefit of typical conservation-agriculture sequestration rates "negligible" — [AGRIS](https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/65df2f3a63b8185d9cab777b); [agshare abstract copy](https://agshare.today/RC/?p=5578). Journal: *European Journal of Soil Science* 69:39–47, from memory; verify. The agshare copy lists 2017 as the online date.
- Lal (2020), "Soil organic matter and water retention", *Agronomy Journal* 112:3265–3277, doi:10.1002/agj2.20282. Only the citation was confirmed; no findings were retrieved — [Lal publication list](https://www.ae-info.org/ae/User/Lal_Rattan/Publications)
- Libohova et al. (likely 2018, *Journal of Soil and Water Conservation*) used the US NCSS characterization database. Silt correlated most with AWHC (r = 0.56) and bulk density correlated negatively (r = −0.34), with the SOM effect varying by texture. This is from an unconfirmed snippet attributed to a co-author's page — [Academia.edu (Wysocki)](https://independent.academia.edu/WysockiD); [SSSA 2017 abstract](https://scisoc.confex.com/crops/2017am/webprogram/Paper108844.html)
- In US Pacific Northwest wheat soils (2023), texture and bulk density were the main AWHC controls and SOC was secondary (snippet) — [ANID record](https://investigadores.anid.cl/es/public_search/work?id=750258)
- Contrasting extension estimate: +1.03% AWC per 1% OM in medium-textured soil (secondary, more optimistic than the meta-analysis) — [UMN Extension](https://blog-crop-news.extension.umn.edu/2020/03/the-connection-between-soil-organic.html)
- Biochar, Razzaghi, Obour & Arthur (2020), *Geoderma* 361:114055, 82 studies from 2010–2019: FC +51% and WP +47% in coarse soils, WP +9% in medium soils, FC essentially unchanged (<1%) in fine soils. Plant-available water rose overall, with soil-type dependence (abstract and EU fiche) — [EU wiki fiche](https://wikis.ec.europa.eu/download/attachments/44158570/Biochar_Impacts_Soil%20water%20retention.pdf?api=v2); [U. Ghana record](https://pure.ug.edu.gh/en/publications/does-biochar-improve-soil-water-retention-a-systematic-review-and-3/)
- Biochar, Edeh, Mašek & Buss (2020), *Science of the Total Environment* 714:136857: biochar significantly increased AWC through increases in both FC and PWP. It increased retention and decreased Ksat in sandy soils, and increased Ksat in clayey soils. Lab AWC responses were on average 9.8% higher than field responses. The largest effects were in coarse soils in lab studies at 30–70 t ha⁻¹ (EU fiche summaries) — [carbondioxide-removal.eu](https://carbondioxide-removal.eu/2020/04/20/edeh-ifeoma-g-et-al-2020-a-meta-analysis-on-biochars-effects-on-soil-water-properties-new-insights-and-future-research-challenges/); [EU wiki fiche](https://wikis.ec.europa.eu/download/attachments/44158174/Soil%20amendment%20with%20biochar_Soil%20water%20retention_7.pdf?api=v2)
- Lentz (2020), *Agronomy Journal* 112(4):2569–2580, reports long-term water retention increases in degraded soils amended with cross-linked polyacrylamide (title only) — [NWISRL eprints](https://eprints.nwisrl.ars.usda.gov/view/id_number/1675.html)
- **PP2019 SOM linkage.** Each power-law PSD parameter is written as b(t, C(t)) = γ_b(t)·b_C(C(t)) (Eq. 14), with a linear SOM term b_C = b₀ + σ_b·C (Eq. 16). The same form is used for R_m and φ. Fitted to Naveed et al. (2014) silt-loam data over C ≈ 40–65 kg m⁻³, the R² values are 0.87 (b), 0.37 (R_m) and 0.94 (φ). The text states that b decreases with SOM (higher retention at a given potential), while R_m and φ increase. (The figure description in the file calls the b trend "positive" in the caption but "negative" in the panel text; the body text supports a negative b–C slope.) — [PP2019 file](/home/user/cavargar/fc_modeling/1-s2.0-S0309170819303124-main.md)
- **PP2019 scenarios.** SOM is driven over 25 years along three trajectories (low input about 52→44, constant about 52, high input about 52→60 kg m⁻³). Higher SOM lowers the wilting point s_w and raises Ksat. After 18 years of no-till, porosity in the high-SOM scenario was still more than 90% of its initial value, which the authors frame as a management trade-off. They caution that the linear fit may not hold outside the data range and that all data are from silt loams — [PP2019 file](/home/user/cavargar/fc_modeling/1-s2.0-S0309170819303124-main.md)

### Inferences
- **Size relative to other drivers.** At typical sequestration rates (tenths of g C kg⁻¹ yr⁻¹), the Minasny–McBratney slope implies FC drifts of well under 1 mm/100 mm per decade. These SOC-driven FC changes are small compared with the post-tillage and W/D changes in the wet range (section 1), except in sands or with large amendment doses such as biochar in coarse soils.
- **Long-term SOC drift.** A time-varying FC parameterized through SOC (as in PP2019) mainly matters for decadal simulations or land-use change. For seasonal simulations, the structural dynamics in section 1 dominate.

### Gaps
- The findings of Lal (2020) and the full numbers of Libohova et al. (2018) were not retrieved.
- No meta-analysis was found that reports the time course of FC after amendment (e.g. biochar ageing or manure decay); most studies are single snapshots.
- No source was found on the long-term manure-rate effects on seasonal physical properties (Jiang et al. 2018, cited in PP2019) beyond the citation.

## 3. Roles of roots, macrofauna, cover crops and W/D cycles in seasonal shifts of the retention curve

### Takeaway
Within a season, W/D cycles are the dominant short-term driver of near-saturated hydraulic properties. Parameters can shift by about an order of magnitude, and the shift is larger near saturation. Biological agents (roots, earthworms, fungi) build continuous macropores and aggregates that raise infiltration (+35% for cover crops, +59% for perennials) and dominate the >0.3 mm pore class. Their direct effect on θ at −33 kPa is weak and slow. Modeling frameworks that couple biota to structure dynamics now exist (Meurer et al. 2020), but these effects are absent from texture-based pedotransfer functions and Earth System Models.

### Cited Findings
- Bodner, Scholl, Loiskandl & Kaul (2013), *Geoderma* 204–205:120–129, doi:10.1016/j.geoderma.2013.04.015, ran a 3-year tension-infiltrometer study comparing cover crops (mustard, rye) with fallow, plus a rotary-harrow disturbance, fitted with Kosugi's lognormal PSD. Hydraulic parameters varied by about an order of magnitude over time, more near saturation than in drier conditions. Rye slightly increased the Kosugi pore-heterogeneity parameter, and other parameters were not significantly affected by cover crop. Seedbed preparation (rotary harrow) substantially increased near-saturated K and macroporosity. W/D cycles were the dominant factor of short-term within-season change (abstract) — [search abstract / PMC3990416](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3990416); [PubMed 24748683](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24748683)
- An earlier EGU abstract reports that cover cropping had "only minor influence" on near-saturated K and the flow-weighted mean pore radius — [EGU2009-13481](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU2009/EGU2009-13481.pdf)
- Basche & DeLonge (2019), *PLOS ONE*, meta-analysed 89 studies of infiltration. Perennials gave +59.2 ± 20.9%, cover crops +34.8 ± 7.7%, and no-till +5.7 ± 9.7% (not significant), while crop grazing gave −21.3 ± 14.9%. Practices with continuous living roots and ground cover were most effective. This study reports infiltration, not FC — [PMC6752860](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6752860/); [UNL DigitalCommons](https://digitalcommons.unl.edu/agronomyfacpub/1280). A companion Basche & DeLonge (2017, SSSAJ) synthesis addressed water storage under "continuous living cover", but its FC numbers were not retrieved — [UNL](https://digitalcommons.unl.edu/agronomyfacpub/1131)
- Jarvis (2007), *European Journal of Soil Science* 58:523–546, reviews non-equilibrium flow in macropores (root and earthworm channels, fissures, interaggregate voids). Pores wider than about 0.3 mm equivalent cylindrical diameter permit rapid non-equilibrium flow. The macropore network is shaped by biota, soil properties, site factors and management (cropping, tillage, traffic). Rain intensity increases macropore flow, while initial water content has complex effects through shrinkage and water repellency — [FOOTPRINT summary](https://sitem.herts.ac.uk/aeru/footprint/pubs/jarvis_2007_foot.htm); [PSU landmark-paper record](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/landmark-papers-no-9-jarvis-nj-2007-a-review-of-non-equilibrium-w/)
- Meurer et al. (2020), "A framework for modelling soil structure dynamics induced by biological activity", *Global Change Biology* 26(10):5382–5403, doi:10.1111/gcb.15289, reviews root growth and faunal and microbial effects on structure. It proposes a model compatible with profile-scale soil–crop models over decades to centuries — [SEI](https://www.sei.org/publications/modelling-soil-structure-dynamics/); [Aberdeen AURA](https://aura.abdn.ac.uk/items/3e6c3640-3a8b-4d71-a14a-c79760f524c7/full)
- Fatichi et al. (2020), *Nature Communications* 11:522, argue that ESM hydraulic parameters come from texture-based pedotransfer functions that miss structure created by biota, and that the bias extends to the sampling methods used to train them. Locally, including structure "significantly alters infiltration-runoff partitioning and recharge in wet and vegetated regions", while global climate effects are masked by coarse resolution — [PMC6985222](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6985222/); [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/5d684711c6564a11a7765b7d4ef8863c)
- PP2019 notes that tilled soils can have more numerous macropores (Heard et al. 1988), but that undisturbed soils' biogenic macropores (earthworms, insects, roots) are more continuous (Ehlers & Van der Ploeg 1976; Wu et al. 1992). PP2019 does not represent W/D or freeze–thaw, and suggests a diffusion term could capture them — [PP2019 file](/home/user/cavargar/fc_modeling/1-s2.0-S0309170819303124-main.md)
- Or et al. describe W/D as a two-sided driver: wetting of dry aggregates fragments them (differential swelling), and drying drives capillary coalescence. In their model coalescence occurs only during drying and stops when the yield stress exceeds the meniscus tensile stress — [or2000.md](/home/user/cavargar/fc_modeling/or2000.md)
- Kreiselmeier et al. (2019) tracked pore dynamics through a winter-wheat cycle under different tillage using a bimodal Kosugi model. Ks correlated with the abundance of transmission pores, and frequent PSD measurement "may be warranted for short time scales" — [UNU record](https://collections.unu.edu/view/UNU:7346)
- Pirlot et al. (2024) list temporary grassland and residue management among the drivers of 3-year retention-curve variation (abstract) — [ORBi PDF](https://orbi.uliege.be/bitstream/2268/321866/1/European%20J%20Soil%20Science%20-%202024%20-%20Pirlot%20-%20How%20does%20soil%20water%20retention%20change%20over%20time%20%20A%20three%e2%80%90year%20field%20study%20under.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Which part of the curve biota affect.** Biogenic macropores (>0.3 mm, i.e. drained at |ψ| < ~1 kPa) mostly affect θs, infiltration and drainage rate. They have little effect on the equilibrium θ at −10/−33 kPa. They do, however, affect the dynamic "drained upper limit" that a field-FC definition measures, i.e. how fast drainage slows. A flux-based FC definition (PP2019, Rodríguez-Iturbe & Porporato) is therefore more sensitive to biota than a potential-based one.
- **Seasonal amplitude.** Seasonal amplitudes of near-saturated parameters (×10 in Bodner et al. 2013) are of the same order as differences between management treatments. A single static calibration may be no better than treatment-agnostic parameters.

### Gaps
- No quantitative seasonal θ(−33 kPa) time series attributable to roots or earthworms alone was found.
- Root-induced pore clogging versus pore creation (e.g. Scholl's BOKU thesis) was not retrievable.
- Freeze–thaw effects were not searched for within this budget.

## 4. How hysteresis and air entrapment affect the apparent FC after wetting versus drying

### Takeaway
At the same suction, the wetting (main or scanning) branch holds less water than the drying branch. Entrapped air keeps wetting-path θ at zero suction at about 85% (±10%) of the drying-path value. FC measured after a wetting event (field drainage following rain or irrigation) is therefore a scanning-curve quantity that depends on the antecedent state, and is generally lower than the main drying curve value used in lab FC (−10/−33 kPa on desorption). Hysteresis also interacts with structure dynamics: air entrapment promotes slaking during fast wetting.

### Cited Findings
- Stonestrom & Rubin (1989), *Water Resources Research*, doi:10.1029/WR025i009p01947, studied two soils. Trapped-air volume rose steadily with water content and was essentially zero during initial drainage from full saturation. Trapping affected retention only above about 60% saturation. Hysteresis did not require trapped air: reversals at about 27% (sand) and 40% (loam) saturation still produced it — [USGS pubs](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70015274); [USGS](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/water-content-dependence-trapped-air-two-soils)
- Likos, Lu & Godt (2014), ASCE *Journal of Geotechnical and Geoenvironmental Engineering*, compiled data showing that wetting-path θ at zero suction averaged about 0.85 (±0.10) of the drying-path value, attributed to air entrapment, with 0.87 for cohesive and 0.81 for cohesionless soils — [USGS](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/hysteresis-and-uncertainty-soil-water-retention-curve-parameters); [USGS pubs](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70048865)
- Or et al. wetted aggregates under tension, or with a fine mist, specifically "to reduce slaking due to air entrapment". They note that aggregate disintegration occurs during infiltration into dry soil even under mist. Their Figure 8b shows the hysteresis-like shift of K(ψ) across W/D cycles — [or2000.md](/home/user/cavargar/fc_modeling/or2000.md)
- Canone, Ferraris, Sander & Haverkamp (2008), *Water Resources Research* 44, doi:10.1029/2008WR007068, interpret field water-retention measurements in relation to hysteresis. The citation is listed in the repo paper `awr.md` (Bovier et al., space-dependent CTRW for soil moisture) — [awr.md](/home/user/cavargar/fc_modeling/awr.md)
- Rudiyanto et al. (2013), SSSAJ, propose a hysteretic dual-porosity hydraulic model. Hysteresis is applied only in the first (structural) pore domain, which mainly affects high water contents, and the second domain is non-hysteretic — [Wiley SSSAJ](https://acsess.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.2136/sssaj2012.0339n)
- Bravo Peña et al. (2025) separated in-situ W/D phases with dθ/dt to fit dynamic retention in a compacted Andosol, treating moisture state and structure dynamics together — [Wiley VZJ](https://acsess.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/vzj2.70021)
- Ahuja et al. (1998) and Zhang et al. (2017) both use drying-branch curves to show consolidation shifts. Zhang found that the shift appeared mainly in the near-saturated W/D cycle, which mixes hysteresis with structural change — [AGRIS (Ahuja)](https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/65df55264c5aef494fe1cf61); [Wiley (Zhang)](https://acsess.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.2136/sssaj2017.01.0038)

### Inferences
- **Size of the hysteresis effect.** Because trapped air matters mostly above about 60% saturation (Stonestrom & Rubin), and FC typically sits at about 50–80% saturation in medium textures, apparent FC after wetting can be biased low relative to lab desorption FC. The bias plausibly ranges from a few percent of θs up to about 15% (the 0.85 ratio is at zero suction, so it is an upper bound at FC). This is an inference, not a measured FC number.
- **Branch-sensitive data.** Field-based FC (sensor drainage curves, as in the repo paper `field_capacity_volumetric_water_content.md`) characterizes a wetting-then-drainage scanning path. Lab FC characterizes the main drying curve. Differences between them partly reflect hysteresis, not error. In soils undergoing structural change, the two effects are confounded, since repeated cycles shift the curve (Or 2000; Zhang 2017).

### Gaps
- No study was found that directly quantifies "FC after wetting versus after drying" at −10/−33 kPa, and no field data were found on the decay of entrapped air during the days after irrigation that would make apparent FC time-dependent.

## 5. Modeling approaches for time-variable hydraulic properties, and what they mean for constant-FC models

### Takeaway
Three families exist:
1. **Stochastic PSD-evolution (kinetic) models.** These include the Fokker–Planck model of Or et al. 2000, the analytical solutions of Leij et al. 2002, its global test by Chandrasekhar et al. 2018/2019 with the SPorDyn code, and the power-law transport model of Pelak & Porporato 2019 with tillage resets and SOM coupling.
2. **Structure-explicit hydraulic models.** These include dual- and multi-porosity or dual-permeability models (HYDRUS dual-porosity, multidomain shrink–swell models, hysteretic dual porosity) and segmental retention models for evolving structure.
3. **Empirical time-variable parameter sets.** These use repeated measurements, as in Schwen et al. 2011.

All of them imply that FC is a state- and history-dependent quantity, not a soil constant. Time-variable parameters measurably improve water-content simulations (relative errors below 13% in Schwen et al. 2011).

### Cited Findings

**Or et al. 2000: Fokker–Planck PSD evolution (primary source)**
- Governing equation (Eq. 3): ∂f/∂t = ½ ∂²[D f]/∂r² − ∂[V f]/∂r − M f. Here V(t,r) is the drift (infinitesimal mean rate of change of pore radius, L T⁻¹), D(t,r) is the diffusion or "infinitesimal variance" (L² T⁻¹), and M(t,r) is first-order pore loss from complete closure (T⁻¹). f is normalized by the initial pore count n₀, so its integral declines as pores close. The coefficients are defined as V = lim Δr̄/Δt, D = lim σ²_Δr/Δt and M = lim (m/n)/Δt (Eqs. 6–8) — [or2000.md](/home/user/cavargar/fc_modeling/or2000.md)
- Initial PSD: lognormal (Eq. 9), f₀ = φ/(√(2π)σr)·exp{−[ln(r/r_m)]²/2σ²}. It can be multimodal (Eq. 10), e.g. bimodal textural plus structural (Nimmo 1997; Durner 1994) — [or2000.md](/home/user/cavargar/fc_modeling/or2000.md)
- Drift from aggregate coalescence (Ghezzehei & Or 2000): unit-cell pore radius R(t) = a{[2(1−h/a)]³ − 4π/3)/(4π/3)}^{1/3} (Eq. 11), porosity φ(t) (Eq. 12), V = (∂R/∂h)(dh/dt) (Eq. 13), applied uniformly to all radii. The rate is set by plastic viscosity η_p(ψ), yield stress τ_y(ψ) and the W/D rate — [or2000.md](/home/user/cavargar/fc_modeling/or2000.md)
- Pore loss (Eq. 15): M = δ(∂φ/∂h)(dh/dt) with δ = Δφ_c/Δφ_v, so total porosity loss is Δφ = (1+δ)Δφ_v. Diffusion (Eq. 16): D = λV, with λ [L] a "pore dispersivity" analogous to solute dispersivity. This is supported only weakly by data showing that PSD variance falls with mean size and porosity (Laliberte & Brooks 1967; Shcherbakov et al. 1995) — [or2000.md](/home/user/cavargar/fc_modeling/or2000.md)
- Simplified constant-coefficient form (Eq. 17), with a zero-flux condition at r = 0 (Eq. 18b) so that all pore disappearance goes through M, solved by explicit finite differences — [or2000.md](/home/user/cavargar/fc_modeling/or2000.md)
- Example parameters (Table 1, Millville silt loam): a = 50 µm, δ = 0.05, λ = 0.50 m, ψ(t) = −5000 − 25t Pa, η_p = 58,000·exp(−7×10⁻⁴ψ) Pa s, τ_y = 40·exp(−7×10⁻⁴ψ) Pa. Structural PSD: r_m = 48 µm, σ² = 0.2, φ₁ = 0.2. Textural PSD: r_m = 7 µm, σ² = 0.8, φ₂ = 0.3 — [or2000.md](/home/user/cavargar/fc_modeling/or2000.md)
- Results (Table 2) over two W/D cycles (0 → 30 → 90 min):
  - Structural mean radius: 49.33 → 43.77 → 38.21 µm.
  - Structural porosity: 0.20 → 0.15 → 0.11, a loss of 45%.
  - Textural porosity (per bulk volume): 0.30 → 0.32 → 0.33.
  - Total porosity: 0.50 → 0.46 → 0.44.
  - Composite PSD variance: 466 → 343 → 239.
  - The bimodality of the retention curve and of K diminishes with each cycle, and θs declines — [or2000.md](/home/user/cavargar/fc_modeling/or2000.md)
- Retention curve from the PSD: ψ = −2γcosβ/(ρ_w g r) (Eq. 19), C(ψ) = f(r) dr/dψ (Eq. 20), θ(ψ) = ∫C dψ + θ_r (Eq. 21). Kozeny–Carman: Ks(t)/Ks(0) = (φ(t)/φ(0))³((1−φ(0))/(1−φ(t)))² (Eq. 22), giving Ks/Ks0 of about 0.7 and 0.55 after cycles 1 and 2. Mualem's model (Eq. 23) is used for K_r. Appendix B gives closed-form porosity evolution assuming the lognormal shape persists (α(t) = ∫V dτ + α(0)) — [or2000.md](/home/user/cavargar/fc_modeling/or2000.md)
- Stated limitations: the D and M terms are conjectural, and the approach "relies on information not readily available". Determining the coefficients from repeated retention-curve measurements is "a distinct possibility" — [or2000.md](/home/user/cavargar/fc_modeling/or2000.md)

**Leij et al. 2002 and Chandrasekhar et al. 2018/2019: analytical solutions and global testing**
- Leij, Ghezzehei & Or (2002a), "Analytical models for soil pore-size distribution after tillage", SSSAJ 66(4):1104–1114, doi:10.2136/sssaj2002.1104, give analytical FPE solutions for drift, dispersion and degradation of a lognormal PSD (abstract) — [Wiley](https://acsess.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.2136/sssaj2002.1104). Leij, Ghezzehei & Or (2002b), "Modeling the dynamics of the soil pore-size distribution", *Soil & Tillage Research* 64:61–78, is the companion paper; Teiwes (1988) data were taken from it in PP2019 — [PP2019 file](/home/user/cavargar/fc_modeling/1-s2.0-S0309170819303124-main.md)
- Chandrasekhar, Kreiselmeier, Schwen, Weninger, Julich, Feger & Schwärzel (2018), "Why we should include soil structural dynamics of agricultural soils in hydrological models", *Water* 10(12):1862, doi:10.3390/w10121862, argue that most hydrological models treat hydraulic properties as time-invariant despite tillage and rainfall effects. **Pelak & Porporato 2019: power-law PSD evolution (primary source)**
- Transport equation without diffusion (Eq. 1): ∂f/∂t = ∂[v f]/∂r − m f. The authors omit the ∂²f/∂r² term because it implies a random walk of individual pore radii "requiring careful justification", though it might represent W/D or freeze–thaw. Tillage is not an integral term; it resets the initial condition — [PP2019 file](/home/user/cavargar/fc_modeling/1-s2.0-S0309170819303124-main.md)
- Power-law PSD (Eqs. 2–5): f = a(t) r^{−b(t)} for 0 ≤ r ≤ R_m(t), with φ = a R_m^{1−b}/(1−b) and b < 1 required. Choosing v = r(a b′ ln r − a′)/(a b) (Eq. 6) and m = (b′/b)(1 + ln r) − a′/(a b) (Eq. 7) keeps the solution a power law at all times (method of characteristics; Appendix A1) — [PP2019 file](/home/user/cavargar/fc_modeling/1-s2.0-S0309170819303124-main.md)
- Hydraulic functions:
  - Retention (Brooks–Corey type), Eq. 9/13: Ψ_s(s,t) = −(C_s/R_m(t))·s^{−1/(1−b(t))}, with C_s = 2σ and s = θ/φ. Air entry is C_s/R_m (Eq. A18).
  - Wilting point (Eq. 10): s_w(t) = (−Ψ_sw R_m(t)/C_s)^{b(t)−1}, with Ψ_sw = −1.5 MPa.
  - Series–parallel conductivity (Eq. 11): K(s,t) = γ_w G_e φ² R_m² (1−b)²/[μ(3−b)(2−b)]·s^{(4−2b)/(1−b)}, with G_e = 1/8.
  - FC is defined dynamically as the s at which K becomes negligible relative to the maximum daily ET (Rodríguez-Iturbe & Porporato 2004) — [PP2019 file](/home/user/cavargar/fc_modeling/1-s2.0-S0309170819303124-main.md)
- Consolidation after tillage (Eq. 15): γ_b(t) = r_b + (1−r_b)·exp{−k_b(t−180)}, where t is days since tillage. It is normalized so that γ = 1 at 180 d (the sampling date in the Teiwes 1988 data, moldboard plough versus 18-year no-till, silt loam), and γ → r_b (the no-till/tilled parameter ratio) as t → ∞. The same form applies to R_m and φ — [PP2019 file](/home/user/cavargar/fc_modeling/1-s2.0-S0309170819303124-main.md)
- Simulated trajectories (7 years of annual tillage, then no-till to 25 years): within the tillage years, b oscillates about 0.82–0.83, R_m about 115–125 µm and φ about 0.38–0.42. Under no-till, b rises monotonically while R_m falls toward about 70–80 µm and φ toward about 0.34–0.36 (values read from the figure descriptions). Tillage lowers s_w and raises Ksat. The authors acknowledge that biogenic macropore continuity under no-till can produce the opposite Ksat effect — [PP2019 file](/home/user/cavargar/fc_modeling/1-s2.0-S0309170819303124-main.md)

The authors argue the approach beats static pedotransfer functions because "it allows for the history of the soil, rather than only its present state", to be accounted for — [PP2019 file](/home/user/cavargar/fc_modeling/1-s2.0-S0309170819303124-main.md)

### Inferences
- **FC is a derived state variable.** In both PSD-evolution models, FC defined at a fixed potential is θ_FC(t) = φ(t)·S(ψ_FC; PSD parameters(t)). Defined as a flux threshold (PP2019), it is s_FC(t) = (K*/K_sat(t))^{(1−b)/(4−2b)}. In both cases FC is a function of the PSD state, which evolves with tillage, consolidation, W/D and SOM. Treating FC as a constant amounts to freezing f(r,t) at one, often unknown, point in the structural cycle.
- **Illustrative PP2019 calculation (my own arithmetic, using values inside the paper's ranges).** With C_s = 0.144 N m⁻¹ and R_m = 120 µm, air entry is about 1.2 kPa. At b = 0.82, s(−33 kPa) = (33/1.2)^{−0.18} ≈ 0.55, which gives θ_FC ≈ 0.23 at φ = 0.42. For a consolidated state (R_m = 80 µm, φ = 0.36, same b), s ≈ 0.59 but θ_FC ≈ 0.21. Under this power-law model, consolidation therefore raises relative saturation at FC but lowers volumetric FC through porosity loss. This differs in sign from Zhang et al.'s (2017) observation of higher θ at a given ψ after W/D, and it shows that the predicted direction of the FC change depends on whether porosity loss is restricted to the largest pores (Or 2000 and Ahuja 1998 style) or spread across the PSD (PP2019's uniform rescaling via φ).
- **Flux-based FC sensitivity.** With the PP2019 exponent (4−2b)/(1−b) ≈ 13, s_FC ∝ K_sat^{−1/13}. A 10-fold Ksat drop after consolidation, comparable to the field-observed near-saturated K declines of 10–100×, would shift s_FC by only about 19%. Flux-defined FC is thus less sensitive to Ksat than to φ and R_m.
- **Practical middle ground.** For bucket or FC-threshold models, a defensible approach is to make FC (and θs) follow an exponential relaxation between tillage events, mirroring PP2019's Eq. 15 or the Leij/Chandrasekhar decay laws, with event-based (W/D-count) rather than calendar time constants. Biochar or SOM terms can be added for multi-year runs, following PP2019's multiplicative structure.

### Gaps
- The explicit exponential-decay expressions and fitted coefficient values from Leij et al. (2002a) and Chandrasekhar et al. (2019) could not be retrieved because the full texts were blocked. The report should cite them generically or after checking the papers.
- No study was found that runs a bucket or FC-threshold model (as opposed to a Richards-equation model) with a time-varying FC and quantifies the error of a constant FC in drainage or ET. This is a genuine literature gap worth stating.
- Pore-network models (e.g. X-ray CT–based networks for evolving structure) were not covered in the searches. No source is listed for them here.
