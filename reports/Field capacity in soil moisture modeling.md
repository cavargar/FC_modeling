# Redefining field capacity for tropical soil-moisture models

Field capacity (FC) is not a soil constant. It is a convention: a threshold on a drainage process that never truly stops. Each model family puts that convention in a different place. Stochastic ecohydrological models define it as a flux ratio (hydraulic conductivity equal to 10% of maximum evapotranspiration). Crop and hydrologic buckets treat it as a hard drainage cap and the top of available water. Richards-equation codes such as HYDRUS never take it as an input at all. Land-surface models use it mainly as the moisture level at which evapotranspiration stress begins. The most consequential finding for practice is that the standard −33 kPa proxy is systematically too dry. Field and flux-based evidence puts in-situ FC near −5 to −10 kPa in temperate soils, near −5 kPa in a Brazilian Oxisol, and about −3 kPa in flux-based maps of Brazilian soils. Common soil-based FC values can underestimate available water by up to 60%. FC also moves over time. Tillage-created pores collapse within a few wetting–drying cycles, hysteresis makes post-rain FC depend on the antecedent state, and organic carbon shifts it slowly over decades. Colombia's two dominant agricultural soil orders break the −33 kPa convention in opposite ways. Andisols lose up to 40% of their water retention irreversibly when air-dried, so legacy laboratory FC values are biased low. Oxisols behave like sands at the wet end, so FC sits near −6 to −10 kPa. For an AGROSAVIA modeller, the practical conclusion is to treat FC as a declared definition–method pair, kept consistent with each model's own drainage formulation and measured on field-moist, undisturbed soil.

## Four definitions share one name: time, suction, flux and curve shape

The concept began as a laboratory shortcut. Briggs and McLane's "moisture equivalent" (1907/1910) was the water retained against 1,000 g in a centrifuge. Veihmeyer and Hendrickson (1931) replaced it with a field idea: the water held "after the excess gravitational water has drained away and after the rate of downward movement of water has materially decreased" ([Twarakavi et al. 2009](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2009wr007944)). Glossaries made this operational as the water remaining **2 or 3 days after wetting, once free drainage is negligible** ([SSSA Glossary](https://www.soils.org/publications/soils-glossary/browse/f?q=publications%2Fsoils-glossary%2Fbrowse%2Ff%2F); [NRCS glossary](https://www.nrcs.usda.gov/sites/default/files/2022-08/A_Glossary_of_Terms_Used_in_Soil_Survey_and_Classification.pdf)). Richards and Weaver (1944) then found that water held at −33 kPa correlated with field capacity in irrigated western-US soils, and −10 kPa in sands. Colman (1947) generalised −1/3 bar to all soils. Assouline and Or note it became "the de facto standard definition of FC despite inherent inconsistencies" ([Assouline & Or 2014](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/2014WR015475)). Regional conventions grew on top of this. The UK uses −5 kPa, following Hall et al. (1977) ([Welsh Government scoping report](https://www.gov.wales/sites/default/files/publications/2021-04/water-storage-capacity-soils-scoping-report.pdf)). Brazilian and European work also uses −6 and −10 kPa ([de Jong van Lier 2017](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/agiwat/v193y2017icp214-220.html)). The choice of suction is therefore partly institutional, not only a matter of texture.

Every modern test finds the −33 kPa proxy too dry. Across 118 Oklahoma Mesonet stations, sensor-derived FC corresponded to median matric potentials of **−7.2 to −7.6 kPa**. Both −10 and −33 kPa underestimated FC, and −33 kPa did so badly ([Krueger & Ochsner 2024](https://acsess.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/saj2.20733)). On USDA-ARS data from 30 US locations, field FC exceeded θ(−33 kPa) by about **0.08 cm³ cm⁻³**, and the two correlated poorly ([Pachepsky et al. 2008](https://scisoc.confex.com/crops/2008am/webprogram/Paper44656.html)). In 22 Brazilian soils, FC measured 48 h after ponding was best predicted by θ at **−6 kPa** ([Ottoni Filho et al. 2014](https://www.rbcsjournal.org/article/revisiting-field-capacity-fc-variation-of-definition-of-fc-and-its-estimation-from-pedotransfer-functions/)).

Since the late 1990s, soil physicists have redefined FC as the water content at which internal drainage falls to a chosen "negligible" flux. Nachabe (1998) argued that FC must be tied to flux, not time, because time to FC depends on initial wetness and wetting depth ([Nachabe 1998](https://ascelibrary.com/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%290733-9437%281998%29124%3A4%28230%29)). Meyer and Gee (1999) took fluxes of 10⁻⁶ to 10⁻⁸ cm s⁻¹ as negligible. In their landfill-cover water budgets, the FC choice alone changed simulated drainage at an arid site **from 25 to 6 mm yr⁻¹** ([Meyer & Gee 1999](https://ascelibrary.com/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%291090-0241%281999%29125%3A7%28595%29)). Twarakavi, Sakai and Šimůnek (2009) simulated free drainage with HYDRUS-1D and a van Genuchten–Mualem (VGM) parameterisation. They defined FC at a default flux of 0.01 cm d⁻¹ (0.1 mm d⁻¹) and derived a closed-form FC in terms of the van Genuchten n and Ks. They concluded that reading FC off the retention curve at benchmark heads "leads to inaccurate results" ([Twarakavi et al. 2009](https://www.pc-progress.com/Documents/Jirka/Twarakavi_et_al_WRR_2009_Field_Capacity.pdf)). The trouble is that "negligible" has no agreed value:

| Source | "Negligible" drainage criterion | Equivalent (mm d⁻¹) |
|---|---|---|
| Meyer & Gee 1999 | 10⁻⁶ – 10⁻⁸ cm s⁻¹ | ≈ 0.86 – 0.009 |
| Nachabe 1998 | 0.05 mm d⁻¹ | 0.05 |
| Twarakavi et al. 2009 (default) | 0.01 cm d⁻¹ | 0.1 |
| Laio et al. 2001 (ecohydrology) | K = 10% of E_max (0.5 cm d⁻¹) | 0.5 |
| Turek et al. 2020, 2022 | 0.25 – 4 mm d⁻¹ | 0.25 – 4 |
| Chen et al. 2024 | 1% of profile storage per day | storage-dependent |

The table spans more than two orders of magnitude. Because K(θ) is steep, especially in coarse soils, that range translates directly into different FC values ([Chen et al. 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0016706124002295); [Turek et al. 2022](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022CEAgr.19406801T/abstract)). The flux definition makes FC consistent with a model's own physics, but it moves the arbitrariness into the threshold rather than removing it.

The fourth family looks for an intrinsic criterion in the shape of the retention curve. Assouline and Or (2014) hypothesise that near-equilibrium hydration with sharply reduced drainage marks the "disruption of hydraulic and liquid phase continuity." They derive a characteristic length from the drainable pore-size distribution and pair it with a time-to-FC criterion; most of their resulting ψ_FC values are wetter than −33 kPa ([Assouline & Or 2014](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/2014WR015475)). A related heuristic is the retention-curve inflection point, linked to Dexter's S-index. It gave the highest FC of all criteria in Turek et al.'s 2019 comparison ([Turek et al. 2019](https://bsssjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/ejss.12747)). The inflection-point, sensor-derived and flux-based estimates all land wetter than −33 kPa, so they agree more with one another than any of them agrees with the convention most databases use. The point is not settled: an EGU abstract disputes that the Assouline–Or criterion relates to drainage-based FC at all ([EGU2015-1826](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU2015/EGU2015-1826.pdf)).

Beneath these definitions, a consensus has formed: FC is a conditional, profile-scale state. Hillel-derived texts note that the wetter the initial profile and the deeper the wetting, "the greater is the apparent field capacity." They also note that FC is "of questionable value for soils having layers of widely differing hydraulic conductivities" ([ScienceDirect Topics: Field Capacity](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/agricultural-and-biological-sciences/field-capacity)). A buried sand layer raises FC in the loam above it, by more when the layer is shallower and thicker ([Chen et al. 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0016706124002295)). A shallow water table converts FC into a boundary-condition quantity. Alizadeh and Mousavi replace s_fc with an "effective field capacity" that depends on water-table depth. They define it as the level "at which soil shifts from the drainage behavior to the capillary uptake condition and vice versa" ([Alizadeh & Mousavi 2013](../alizadeh2013.md)). FC as the upper bound of plant-available water is also contested. De Jong van Lier found that **10–50% of transpired water** is taken up at water contents above FC ([de Jong van Lier 2017](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/agiwat/v193y2017icp214-220.html)).

## Estimation methods disagree by up to 0.08 cm³ cm⁻³ before instrument error enters

Disagreement among FC methods is mostly definitional, not instrumental. The 0.08 cm³ cm⁻³ gap between θ(−33 kPa) and field FC is as large as the typical error of a texture-only pedotransfer function. A perfectly measured −33 kPa point can therefore miss field FC by as much as a poor PTF does. Regression-tree corrections that take texture and measured θ33 as inputs and target field FC "greatly reduced" texture-specific bias ([Nemes et al. 2010](https://old.iuss.org/19th%20WCSS/Symposium/pdf/0021.pdf)). An Indian study found r ≥ 0.9 between laboratory and in-situ FC at both 10 and 33 kPa, with a systematic offset that regression could remove ([Indian J. Agric. Sci.](https://epubs.icar.org.in/index.php/IJAgS/article/view/30114)). A handful of field FC values per soil can therefore calibrate a large laboratory archive. Pressure-plate equilibration errors, by contrast, are documented mainly below about −200 kPa and in fine-textured soils ([Bittelli & Flury 2009](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Errors-in-Water-Retention-Curves-Determined/99900547678601842); [Solone et al. 2012](https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/65de57820f3e94b9e5cdde1b)). They matter more for the wilting point than for FC.

The field drainage test is the nominal reference, but its outcome depends on the process and the protocol. The plot is wetted, covered to stop evaporation, and read once drainage is "negligible." That takes one night in Florida sands ([Bean et al. 2018](../field_capacity_volumetric_water_content.md)), 24–48 h in the repo's HYDRUS study ([Liu et al. 2025](../agronomy_hydrus1d_2018.md)), and 1–10 days in Mesonet records depending on texture ([OSU Extension](https://extension.okstate.edu/fact-sheets/determining-field-capacity-using-continuous-soil-water-content-data.html)). Heavy soils can take weeks ([APSIM/DNRME 2017](https://www.apsim.info/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/Methods-for-PAWWRC-estimation_DNRME.pdf)). Oklahoma State's guidance also warns that FC estimated from sensor records "can vary depending on the type of sensor used" ([OSU Extension](https://extension.okstate.edu/fact-sheets/determining-field-capacity-using-continuous-soil-water-content-data)).

Bean, Huffaker and Migliaccio (2018) are the repo's key methodological contribution. They automated FC extraction from hourly sensor θ in two steps. First, they labelled valid wetting–drying cycles using a physics-based peak detector (SWDP), R `findpeaks`, a symbolic-aggregation classifier (SAX) and density histograms. Second, they estimated FC on each valid cycle. One estimator takes the "knee" where dθ/dt drops sharply. Another takes the `findpeaks` plateau. A third fits the overnight decline θ(t) = (θB − θFC)e^(−λt) + θFC and extrapolates to t → ∞. SAX labelled cycles best (accuracy 0.68–0.90). All FC estimators "were typically within 0.03" cm³ cm⁻³ of the expert values. The biases were systematic: knee and plateau estimates ran high, and regression estimates ran low ([Bean et al. 2018](../field_capacity_volumetric_water_content.md)). The more important result is conceptual. FC changed from cycle to cycle, typically by less than 2% per day and by up to 6–7% between events. The expert-labelled FC in a gravelly loam ranged from 0.14 to 0.48, which the authors attribute to antecedent moisture, preferential flow and hysteresis. Two caveats limit how far this transfers. The "truth" was itself a convention (one night of drainage), and only coarse, well-drained soils without water tables were tested. The authors warn that finer soils "may produce less promising results."

Pedotransfer functions trade accuracy for coverage. Rosetta's water-retention RMSE falls from **0.078 to 0.044 cm³ cm⁻³** as inputs are added, from textural class alone up to bulk density plus θ33 and θ1500 ([Schaap et al. 2001](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/222687859_ROSETTA_A_computer_program_for_estimating_soil_hydraulic_parameters_with_hierarchical_pedotransfer_functions)). Rosetta3 improves on this only modestly ([Zhang & Schaap 2017](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017JHyd..547...39Z/abstract)). An independent test of the EU-PTFs gave an FC RMSE of 0.033 cm³ cm⁻³ ([CROSBI record](https://www.bib.irb.hr/1215156)). Machine-learning PTFs do not automatically win. On a 223-sample Iranian dataset, random forest was less reliable than nonlinear regression ([J. Areeo](https://journals.areeo.ac.ir/article_100011.html)). Every PTF also reproduces its training definition, usually θ at a fixed suction, and so inherits the −33 kPa bias. Gridded products such as HiHydroSoil v2.0 chain SoilGrids map error onto PTF error and publish no FC accuracy statistics ([FutureWater 2020](https://www.futurewater.nl/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/HiHydroSoil-v2.0-High-Resolution-Soil-Maps-of-Global-Hydraulic-Properties_v2.pdf)).

Inverse and remote-sensing approaches constrain behaviour more than FC itself. Liu et al. inverse-estimated VGM parameters layer by layer in HYDRUS-1D, using measured FC as a constraint. They reached calibration R² ≈ 0.81 and validation R² of 0.74–0.88, but never checked whether the fitted curves reproduce the measured FC ([Liu et al. 2025](../agronomy_hydrus1d_2018.md)). SMAP drydown analysis fits exponential decay to post-rain surface moisture. Drying timescales are shorter in arid and sandier regions, with only a "relatively minor" texture effect ([McColl et al. 2017, via HESS 2018](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/22/1649/2018/)). It yields footprint-scale timescales for the top ~5 cm, not an agronomic FC. One empirical warning stands out: a large mountain network found the moisture threshold for drainage onset "significantly lower than the commonly employed field capacity metric in hydrological models" ([DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/014871c157e241738ed49c75cddf83bf)).

| Method | Data needs | Typical accuracy or bias | Best use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lab θ at −33 kPa | Cores, pressure plate | ~0.08 below field FC (US data) | Legacy databases, US convention |
| Lab θ at −6/−10 kPa | Undisturbed cores, sandbox or plate | Closest lab proxy to field FC in many soils | Agronomic FC without field data |
| Field drainage test | Plot, θ and ψ sensors, 2–10+ days | Reference by definition, but depends on time, threshold and layering | Benchmarking other methods |
| Sensor time-series algorithms | ≥1 season of hourly θ plus rain/irrigation log | ~0.03 of expert convention (coarse soils); captures dynamics | Irrigation scheduling, site models |
| PTFs | Texture ± OM, BD, retention points | RMSE ~0.03–0.08, plus definitional bias | Regional models, data-poor sites |
| Inverse modelling | θ series plus forcing | Fit RMSE ~0.02 (not FC error); equifinality | Richards models needing consistent K(θ) |
| Satellite drydowns | Public SMAP data | ~5 cm depth, 9–36 km scale | Land-surface model calibration, data assimilation |

## Each model family hides field capacity in a different parameter

### Stochastic and bucket ecohydrology: FC is a leakage-onset flux ratio

In the Rodríguez-Iturbe–Porporato–Laio lineage, s_fc is the lower boundary of the leakage regime in a piecewise loss function. Below s_fc, losses are evapotranspiration only. Between s* and s_fc, ET equals E_max with no drainage. Above s_fc, leakage rises exponentially to Ks at saturation: L(s) = Ks[e^{β(s−s_fc)} − 1]/[e^{β(1−s_fc)} − 1], with β = 2b + 4 ranging from 12 for sand to 26 for clay. Laio et al. define s_fc "operationally" as the moisture at which "hydraulic conductivity becomes negligible (here 10%) compared to the maximum daily evapotranspiration losses E_max (… assumed to be 0.5 cm/d)." That puts K(s_fc) = 0.5 mm d⁻¹, a flux criterion and not a matric potential ([Laio et al. 2001](../1-s2.0-S0309170801000057-main.md)). By contrast, they fix the hygroscopic point, the wilting point and the stomatal-closure point s* at −10, −3 and −0.03 MPa. One implication is rarely noticed. Because the criterion compares K with E_max, s_fc is partly a climate and vegetation parameter: a higher E_max lowers s_fc in the same soil.

Two checks on Laio's Table 1 matter for anyone reusing it; both are our own recalculations from the paper's equations. First, evaluating its Clapp–Hornberger retention curves gives potentials at the tabulated s_fc of about −24 kPa (sand), −3 kPa (loamy sand), −12 kPa (sandy loam), −15 kPa (loam) and −2 kPa (clay). The conventional −33 kPa point falls at s ≈ 0.32, 0.30, 0.46, 0.56 and 0.78, essentially equal to Laio's s*. In this parameterisation the agronomic FC coincides with the onset of stomatal closure. A modeller who substitutes a −33 kPa FC for s_fc therefore collapses the well-watered plateau between s* and s_fc and starts leakage too early. Second, inverting Ks·s^c = 0.05 cm d⁻¹ reproduces the loamy-sand, sandy-loam and loam entries (0.52, 0.56, 0.65). It does not reproduce sand or clay. Sand at Ks = 200 cm d⁻¹ gives 0.47, not 0.35, and clay at 10 cm d⁻¹ gives 0.81, not ≈1. Those two entries rest on unstated Ks values or on judgement and should not be reused uncritically.

The lineage has three drainage forms. Rodríguez-Iturbe et al. (1999) used a linear leakage decay. Laio et al. (2001) used the exponential form above. The "minimalist" models of Milly (1993), Porporato et al. (2004) and Daly and Porporato (2006) drain instantaneously above a threshold s_1 "comprised between field capacity and saturation." In the minimalist form, FC enters only through the storage capacity w_0 = nZ_r(s_1 − s_w). The soil-moisture distribution becomes a truncated Gamma governed by the dryness index and γ = w_0/α ([Daly & Porporato 2006](../Water%20Resources%20Research%20-%202006%20-%20Daly%20-%20Impact%20of%20hydroclimatic%20fluctuations%20on%20the%20soil%20water%20balance.md)). Shifting s_1 is therefore mathematically identical to changing rooting depth. Other repo models drop FC entirely. Rigby and Porporato use continuous Brooks–Corey percolation k_s s^c at all moisture levels ([Rigby & Porporato 2006](../hess-10-861-2006.md)). Bovier et al.'s space-dependent continuous-time random walk has no FC parameter ([Bovier et al.](../awr.md)). Gandhi et al.'s flow-kick vegetation model has no drainage term at all ([Gandhi et al. 2025](../2501.01569v1.md)). Earlier, Manabe's 1969 land-surface bucket set FC as a hard 15 cm ceiling in a 1 m layer, with overflow as runoff and ET reduced below 75% of FC ([AMS secondary source](https://ams.confex.com/ams/pdfpapers/72752.pdf)).

How much does s_fc matter? No published analytical sensitivity study was found. We solved Laio's steady-state equations for a loam (Z_r = 30 cm, α = 1.5 cm, E_max = 0.45 cm d⁻¹) as an illustration. Raising s_fc from 0.57 to 0.80 cut the leakage fraction from 0.13 to 0.07 of rainfall in a dry climate (λ = 0.2 d⁻¹), a drop of roughly 40%. It cut leakage from 0.39 to 0.32 in a wet climate (λ = 0.5 d⁻¹). ET/R rose by only 0.02–0.05. Mean moisture changed by 0.02–0.07. Stress probability halved only in the wet case, where probability mass sits between s* and s_fc. Part of the "saved" leakage reappeared as saturation-excess runoff. The pattern is the one that matters: **s_fc errors propagate mainly into recharge and leaching, and much less into ET**. That matches crop-model evidence below. Irrigation applications raise the stakes further. In traditional schemes, irrigation refills to FC ([Vico & Porporato 2010](https://dukespace.lib.duke.edu/items/5efe097e-1e84-461d-9328-44976c3257ba)). Alizadeh and Mousavi found about 30% of infiltrated irrigation percolating below the root zone under stress-avoidance irrigation of barley ([Alizadeh & Mousavi 2013](../alizadeh2013.md)). In inverse applications, FC and wilting point "don't match conventional definitions because the model simplifies soil water loss." They are fitted parameters, not measured ones ([Bassiouni et al. 2018](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/22/3229/2018/hess-22-3229-2018.pdf)).

The main structural critique is vertical averaging. A bucket "cannot represent the migration of a wetting front" and over-predicts uptake right after rain ([Guswa et al. 2002](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1029/2001WR000826)). Rigby and Porporato add that leakage during a storm is "of equal or more concern … than is runoff" for simplified models. That argues for finite-rate drainage terms over the instantaneous s_1 form ([Rigby & Porporato 2006](../hess-10-861-2006.md)). Romano et al. found that a bucket model agrees best with the Richards-based SWAP model when θ_fc comes from a drainage method rather than a fixed retention point ([Romano et al. 2011](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/15/3877/2011/hess-15-3877-2011.pdf)).

### Richards-equation models: FC is emergent, so the error moves into α, n and Ks

HYDRUS, SWAP and SWIM never take FC as an input. It emerges from the retention and conductivity functions and the boundary conditions, and studies extract it either at a benchmark head or by simulating drainage to a flux criterion. Panthi et al. coupled AquaCrop to a Richards solver and set θ_fc per texture with Twarakavi's q_fc = 0.1 mm d⁻¹ ([Panthi et al. 2025](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/ird.70172)). The repo's HYDRUS-1D study shows both the promise and the pitfalls. Liu et al. measured layer-specific FC from post-storm sensor data: 29.0–40.7% in a tea plantation and 22.5–28.6% in adjacent grassland on the same Luvisol ([Liu et al. 2025](../agronomy_hydrus1d_2018.md)). Evaluating their fitted VGM parameters (our calculation) shows the measured FC matches θ(−33 kPa) in the tea soil but θ(−10 kPa) or wetter in the grassland topsoil. The matric potential at "field FC" therefore differs by land use and depth on one soil type.

The fitted tea parameters raise a further flag. Very low n (1.07–1.18) combined with Ks of 112–384 cm d⁻¹ implies an available water of only about 0.05–0.13 cm³ cm⁻³. That fit reproduces water-content dynamics (R² ≈ 0.8) while carrying physically questionable FC and available water. It is a textbook case of equifinality in the inverse problem, made worse by the backfilled soil. The paper also has internal inconsistencies a reviewer should note. The FC method requires rainfall above 80 mm, yet the reported maximum daily rainfall is 26.7 mm. The FC ranges differ between the Results and the Conclusions. A promised data-assimilation step is never performed ([Liu et al. 2025](../agronomy_hydrus1d_2018.md)).

### Crop models: FC is a cap and a reservoir at once, but yields are buffered

FAO-56, AquaCrop, DSSAT, APSIM-SoilWat, CropSyst, STICS and WOFOST are cascading buckets in which FC (called the drained upper limit, DUL, in DSSAT and APSIM) does two jobs. It is the drainage threshold, and it is the top of total available water, TAW = 1000(θFC − θWP)Z_r ([FAO-56 Ch. 8](https://www.fao.org/3/x0490e/x0490e0e.htm)). Water above FC drains at a rate set by an empirical coefficient. DSSAT and APSIM-SoilWat use SWCON, the fraction of water above DUL draining per day, which ranges from about 0.8 for excessively drained to 0.005 for very poorly drained soils ([Shelia et al. 2018](https://www.pc-progress.com/Documents/Jirka/JHH/Shelia_et_al_JHH_2018.pdf); [APSIM SoilWat](https://www.apsim.info/documentation/model-documentation/soil-modules-documentation/soilwat/); [UNFCCC DSSAT calibration](https://unfccc.int/files/national_reports/non-annex_i_natcom/cge/application/pdf/agriculture.dssatvalidation.pdf)). AquaCrop uses a drainage coefficient τ derived from Ksat, roughly 0.2 for clay and 0.9 for sand ([FAO AquaCrop manual](https://www.fao.org/fileadmin/user_upload/faowater/docs/AquaCropChapter2.pdf)). These coefficients function as calibration knobs that absorb the mismatch between a hard threshold and real, gradual drainage. S-map work derived APSIM SWCON values by matching SWIM3 Richards drainage, which concedes the point ([S-map parameters for APSIM](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/358399559_S-map_parameters_for_APSIM)).

Sensitivity evidence points the same way as the stochastic models. A PAWC error of up to 20 mm (roughly ±0.02 cm³ cm⁻³ over 1 m) changed water-limited wheat yield by **under 200 kg ha⁻¹** in APSIM ([Field Crops Research](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S037837741831432X)). In STICS, soil modules drove drainage and leaching far more than yield ([Ruget et al. 2002](https://www.agronomy-journal.org/articles/agro/abs/2002/02/04/04.html)). In an APSIM structural ensemble on Ferralsols, Alisols and Chernozems, the crop model explained more than 60% of output variance, and SoilWat versus SWIM3 was a secondary source ([Chapagain et al. 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/agronomy/articles/10.3389/fagro.2023.1213074/full)). The sharpest result comes from the WOFOST-versus-SWAP benchmark. To match the Richards model, the bucket needed an FC **more than one pF unit wetter** than common soil-based values. Those common values underestimated available water by up to 60%, yet simulated production was fairly insensitive ([Turek et al. 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0168169922001181)). Buckets and Richards solvers agree under free drainage: DSSAT's bucket and DSSAT–HYDRUS gave water-content agreement indices of 0.9 and 0.8 and pod-yield agreement of 0.94–0.99 ([Shelia et al. 2018](https://www.pc-progress.com/Documents/Jirka/JHH/Shelia_et_al_JHH_2018.pdf)). They diverge with shallow water tables, during post-rain redistribution and in layered profiles. EPIC's bucket rarely let moisture exceed FC, which mischaracterised denitrification ([Longo et al. 2021](https://acsess.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/vzj2.20142)).

### Hydrologic and land-surface models: FC mostly sets when ET becomes water-limited

SWAT uses FC at −33 kPa as a percolation threshold. Only water above FC drains, with travel time TT_perc = (SAT − FC)/Ksat ([SWAT 2009 Theory](https://swat.tamu.edu/media/99192/swat2009-theory.pdf)). The land-surface models mainly use FC as a stress reference, because their drainage runs through Clapp–Hornberger or Campbell conductivity. VIC's critical point Wcr is about 70% of FC, with FC at −33 kPa ([VIC documentation](https://github.com/UW-Hydro/VIC/blob/master/docs/Documentation/Definitions.md)). Noah and Noah-MP use the reference moisture SMCREF, labelled "field capacity," below which transpiration is stressed. It was added to the Cosby et al. (1984) texture tables only later, by Chen and Dudhia (2001), and table versions differ ([Noah-MP technote](https://www.jsg.utexas.edu/noah-mp/files/Noah-MP_Technote_v0.2.pdf); [Chen & Dudhia 2001](https://www2.mmm.ucar.edu/people/dudhia/files/papers/chendudhia101.pdf)). JULES computes its stress factor between θwilt and θcrit, which it treats as FC ([JULES β source](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/JULES-soil-moisture-stress-factor-b-with-p00-solid-line-and-p003-dashed-line-The_fig1_334672145)). These defaults are not harmless. When Noah-MP's tables were replaced with measured Texas data, **FC was the soil parameter with the largest influence on ET** ([Yimam et al. 2025](https://acsess.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/agg2.70048)). Sites with underestimated FC consistently underestimated soil moisture ([HESS 2025](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/29/2551/2025/)). Texture-class tables also cannot represent the land-use contrast Liu et al. measured on a single soil type.

| Model family | Where FC lives | Typical source of value | Outputs most sensitive to FC error |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stochastic ecohydrology (Laio, Daly) | Leakage onset s_fc or drainage threshold s_1 | K(s) = 0.1·E_max; fitted by inversion | Leakage and recharge; wet-climate stress |
| Water-table stochastic models (Alizadeh) | Effective FC s_e(Z) | Water-table depth plus Brooks–Corey parameters | Capillary supply, irrigation percolation |
| Richards models (HYDRUS, SWAP) | Emergent from VGM plus boundary conditions | Flux criterion or benchmark head after fitting | Fitted α, n, Ks; available water |
| Crop buckets (FAO-56, AquaCrop, DSSAT, APSIM) | Drainage cap and TAW top | −33 kPa, DUL field test, PTF | Drainage, N leaching, irrigation timing; yield less so |
| SWAT | Percolation threshold | −33 kPa per layer | Percolation, baseflow |
| Land-surface models (VIC, Noah-MP, JULES) | ET-stress reference | Cosby/Chen–Dudhia tables | Latent heat, soil-moisture memory |

## Pore structure moves field capacity on event, seasonal and decadal clocks

FC drifts because the pore-size distribution drifts, and the drift is concentrated in the wet range of the retention curve, exactly where FC sits. Or et al. treat the interaggregate pore space "corresponding roughly to the 0–33 kPa soil water retention range" as the fraction most affected by tillage ([Or et al. 2000](../or2000.md)). Ahuja et al. found that tillage changes only the range between the air-entry value and about ten times it ([Ahuja et al. 1998](https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/65df55264c5aef494fe1cf61)). The capillary equation shows why this matters for FC definitions (our calculation). −10 kPa drains pores down to ~14 µm radius, and −33 kPa down to ~4.4 µm. The structural pore median in Or et al.'s example is ~48 µm. **θ at −10 kPa should therefore be far more time-variable than θ at −33 kPa**, so adopting the wetter, more realistic suction also makes FC more dynamic.

The magnitudes are large and fast. In Or et al.'s Fokker–Planck simulation of a Millville silt loam, two wetting–drying cycles cut structural porosity from 0.20 to 0.11 (−45%). Mean structural radius fell from 49 to 38 µm, and Ks dropped to about 0.55 of its initial value ([Or et al. 2000](../or2000.md)). In two Hawaiian soils, near-saturated K fell **100-fold** with wetting–drying after tillage, and retention changed after a single cycle ([Mapa et al. 1986](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1986SSASJ..50.1133M/abstract)). In another tilled layer, porosity stabilised after four cycles. The retention curve shifted toward higher water content at a given potential, mostly in the cycle that approached saturation ([Zhang et al. 2017](https://acsess.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.2136/sssaj2017.01.0038)). That last result suggests the clock for FC evolution should count wetting events, not calendar days. Within a season, hydraulic parameters vary by about an order of magnitude near saturation, with wetting–drying the dominant short-term driver ([Bodner et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3990416)). In a three-year field study, retention curves "varied considerably over time and between systems." The authors note such dynamics "are rarely taken into account in models" ([Pirlot et al. 2024](https://orbi.uliege.be/bitstream/2268/321866/1/European%20J%20Soil%20Science%20-%202024%20-%20Pirlot%20-%20How%20does%20soil%20water%20retention%20change%20over%20time%20%20A%20three%e2%80%90year%20field%20study%20under.pdf)).

The direction of the FC change is not obvious, and models disagree on it. When consolidation removes only the largest pores, volumetric θ at −33 kPa can stay constant or rise as the bulk volume shrinks; textural porosity per bulk volume rises from 0.30 to 0.33 in Or et al.'s Table 2. Pelak and Porporato instead rescale the whole power-law distribution. In an illustrative calculation with their equations, consolidation raises relative saturation at −33 kPa (0.55 to 0.59) while volumetric FC falls (0.23 to 0.21) through porosity loss ([Pelak & Porporato 2019](../1-s2.0-S0309170819303124-main.md)). The sign of the predicted FC change therefore depends on whether pore loss is confined to the structural domain. That assumption should be tested before such models are used for Colombian soils.

Hysteresis adds a second, faster form of non-uniqueness. FC measured in the field after rain or irrigation lies on a wetting-then-drainage scanning path. Laboratory FC lies on the main drying curve. Entrapped air keeps wetting-branch θ at zero suction at about **0.85 (±0.10) of the drying-branch value** ([Likos et al. 2014](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/hysteresis-and-uncertainty-soil-water-retention-curve-parameters)). Trapped air affects retention only above about 60% saturation ([Stonestrom & Rubin 1989](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70015274)). Part of the gap between sensor FC and laboratory FC is therefore hysteresis, not error. Bean et al.'s finding that FC fell after drier antecedent conditions is consistent with this ([Bean et al. 2018](../field_capacity_volumetric_water_content.md)).

Organic carbon moves FC far more slowly and less than is often claimed. A meta-analysis of 60 studies plus more than 50,000 measurements found that +10 g C kg⁻¹ raises FC by 1.61 and available water by only **1.16 mm per 100 mm of soil**. The effect is largest in sands, and the authors call the gain from typical sequestration "negligible" ([Minasny & McBratney 2018](https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/65df2f3a63b8185d9cab777b)). Biochar is the exception in coarse soils, where it raises FC by about 51% and has essentially no effect in fine soils ([Razzaghi et al. 2020](https://wikis.ec.europa.eu/download/attachments/44158570/Biochar_Impacts_Soil%20water%20retention.pdf?api=v2)). Biota mostly build pores wider than about 0.3 mm ([Jarvis 2007](https://sitem.herts.ac.uk/aeru/footprint/pubs/jarvis_2007_foot.htm)), which drain at suctions below about 1 kPa. They change infiltration (+35% under cover crops, +59% under perennials) more than the equilibrium θ at FC ([Basche & DeLonge 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6752860/)). They do, however, change how fast drainage slows, so a flux-based FC is more sensitive to biota than a suction-based one. Earth-system models miss this structure entirely, because their texture-based PTFs are trained on samples that exclude it ([Fatichi et al. 2020](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6985222/)).

Three modelling families now represent this dynamism. Stochastic pore-size evolution includes Or et al.'s Fokker–Planck model with drift, dispersion and pore-loss terms ([Or et al. 2000](../or2000.md)), its analytical solutions and global tests ([Chandrasekhar et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.3390/w10121862)), and Pelak and Porporato's power-law transport model. That model has tillage resets, exponential consolidation γ(t) = r_b + (1 − r_b)e^{−k_b(t−180)}, and a linear SOM coupling. It defines FC dynamically as the s at which K is negligible relative to maximum ET ([Pelak & Porporato 2019](../1-s2.0-S0309170819303124-main.md)). The flux-defined FC turns out to be fairly robust: in their model s_FC scales as Ksat^(−1/13), so a 10-fold Ksat drop shifts it by only ~19% (our calculation). Structure-explicit models, including dual-porosity, hysteretic dual-porosity and shrink–swell variants, form the second family. Empirical time-variable parameter sets form the third, and time-variable parameters improved Richards simulations of tilled layers to relative errors below 13% ([Schwen et al. 2011](https://sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0378377411001879)). A genuine gap remains: no study was found that runs a bucket or FC-threshold model with time-varying FC and quantifies the drainage or ET error of holding FC constant.

## Andisols and Oxisols break the −33 kPa convention in opposite ways

### Andisols: irreversible drying biases laboratory FC low

The extremely high water retention of Andean Andisols comes from short-range-order minerals (allophane, imogolite). In non-allophanic and páramo soils it also comes from organic colloids ([Buytaert et al. 2006](https://condesan.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/06/Articulo2.pdf)). The defining problem for FC is irreversibility. "Drying these soils induces structural changes and hydrophobicity in the organic matter. The result is an irreversible decrease of the water retention, up to 40%" ([Buytaert et al. 2006](https://condesan.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/06/Articulo2.pdf)). A study of Ecuadorian Umbric Andosols reported a 40% retention loss from laboratory air-drying. It judged organic matter "more important than allophane clay content" in controlling retention ([SUM 2002](https://paramo.cc.ic.ac.uk/pubs/SUM.pdf)). Desiccation of Guadeloupe Andosols from 1 to 1,550 kPa removed **50% of void space**, while a neighbouring Nitisol barely shrank ([Dorel et al. 2000](https://bsssjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1475-2743.2000.tb00188.x)). The WRB hydric criterion is defined on undried samples for exactly this reason ([FAO Andosol notes](https://www.eweb.unex.es/eweb/edafo/FAO/Andosol.htm)).

Colombian practice still sometimes runs pressure membranes at 33 and 1,500 kPa on air-dried samples ([Redalyc Andisol study](https://www.redalyc.org/pdf/573/57311108002.pdf)). Cenicafé measures FC at 0.33 bar ([Lince-Salazar & Sadeghian 2019](https://publicaciones.cenicafe.org/index.php/cenicafe/article/view/3287)). Any air-dried legacy value is therefore likely biased low, plausibly by tens of percent, and should not parameterise AquaCrop or DSSAT without correction. Coffee-zone soils show gravimetric FC from **27.1% to 53.8%**, highest in volcanic-ash soils ([UNAD RIAA](https://portal.amelica.org/ameli/journal/130/1303815003/html/)). With Andic bulk densities below 0.9 Mg m⁻³ ([RFNAM 62(1)](http://www.scielo.org.co/pdf/rfnam/v62n1/a17v62n1.pdf)), a 54% gravimetric FC at 0.7 Mg m⁻³ is only about 0.38 cm³ cm⁻³ volumetric (our arithmetic). Models need volumetric input, so the conversion must use bulk density measured at the same moisture state.

Texture-only PTFs such as Rosetta (sand–silt–clay input) and Saxton–Rawls are ill-suited to Andisols on two counts. The real drivers (allophane, Al/Fe–humus complexes, organic carbon) are missing from the inputs, and particle-size analysis itself is unreliable because these soils disperse poorly. No Colombian Andisol retention PTF was found. The nearest analogues are Mexican Andosol PTF validations ([INIFAP](https://cienciasforestales.inifap.gob.mx/index.php/forestales/article/view/666)) and Dorel et al.'s model, which predicts macroporosity from allophane and organic-matter content ([CIRAD thesis](https://agritrop.cirad.fr/564041/1/document_564041.pdf)). Hydrologically, Andisols on slopes show slow moisture declines and high retention deep in the Bw horizon ([RFNAM](https://revistas.unal.edu.co/index.php/refame/article/download/36490/46747?inline=1)). Land-use change in páramo catchments causes irreversible structural loss that reduces both peak and low flows ([Catena 2021](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0341816221000862)). For an Andisol, therefore, FC is not even stable across land-use history. Recent in-situ work on a compacted Andosol separates wetting and drying phases with dθ/dt to fit dynamic bimodal retention ([Bravo Peña et al. 2025](https://acsess.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/vzj2.70021)), a template for Colombian sensor sites.

### Oxisols: clay mineralogy, sand-like drainage, FC near −6 to −10 kPa

Strongly weathered Oxisols are microaggregated into stable granules. Their retention is "typical of a clay at low matric potentials" while they "resemble sands at high matric potentials." Their textures often fall "outside the range of validity" of temperate PTFs, whose estimates "may also be significantly in error, or they may fail totally" ([Tomasella et al. 2000, citing Tomasella & Hodnett 1998](https://acsess.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.2136/sssaj2000.641327x)). They drain quickly, so FC arrives at low suction. A flux criterion of 1 mm d⁻¹ placed FC in a Brazilian Oxisol at about **−5.4 kPa** ([de Jong van Lier & Wendroth 2016](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/reexamination-of-the-field-capacity-concept-in-a-brazilian-oxisol/)). Mato Grosso work recommends 10 kPa on undisturbed samples ([RBEAA](http://www.scielo.br/j/rbeaa/a/rVLNhmgQnMYMcm4FWyVxNWn/?lang=en)). Van den Berg et al. set FC at −10 kPa for their Ferralsol PTFs ([van den Berg et al. 1997](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0016706197000451)). At national scale, Turek et al. used SoilGrids-based PTFs and SWAP drainage simulations to map FC across Brazil. With a 4 mm d⁻¹ criterion, FC arrives after 2 ± 1 days at about **−3 kPa**, and fixed-suction estimates underestimate the FC head **two- to almost four-fold** ([Turek et al. 2020](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0016706120301300)). The tropical PTF toolkit is correspondingly distinct. It includes Hodnett and Tomasella's VG PTFs from the IGBP-DIS tropical subset ([Hodnett & Tomasella 2002](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/222514532_Marked_differences_between_van_Genuchten_soil_water-retention_parameters_for_temperate_and_tropical_soils_A_new_water-retention_pedo-transfer_functions_developed_for_tropical_soils)) and the HYBRAS database of 445 profiles. Its Brazilian PTFs "performed better than the temperate models" ([Ottoni et al. 2018](https://acsess.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.2136/vzj2017.05.0095)). In Colombia, a Rosetta-type Ksat indicator "had no application" on four Valle del Cauca soils ([Lozano et al. 2005](https://revistas.unal.edu.co/index.php/acta_agronomica/article/view/113)).

AGROSAVIA's Altillanura work is already ahead of the −33 kPa default. Its Taluma station study computed available water between **10 and 1,500 kPa** ([Frontiers 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/environmental-science/articles/10.3389/fenvs.2022.687804/full)). The laboratory's standard five-point curve includes 10 kPa ([AGROSAVIA VIVO](https://vivo.agrosavia.co/display/serana4)). Altillanura Oxisols are kaolinitic, weakly structured and prone to sealing and compaction under excessive tillage ([AGROSAVIA](https://repository.agrosavia.co/items/6101a8a9-9b32-4ab2-893d-f9866e5a4d02)). That profile is exactly where the post-tillage dynamics described above should be strongest. No Colombian in-situ drainage test on Oxisols was found. A 2026 UNAL thesis monitoring moisture for 12 months at eight Meta–Vichada sites is the most promising emerging dataset ([UNAL repository](https://repositorio.unal.edu.co/collections/af38aa88-f50a-4238-a3e4-73e7da6d590c)).

### Climate regime decides which FC error hurts

Colombia's rainfall regime determines which FC error matters. The Andean region is bimodal, and the Orinoquia, Amazon, Pacific and most of the Caribbean are unimodal ([Estacionalidad de la lluvia en Colombia](https://researchgate.net/publication/309538732_Estacionalidad_de_la_lluvia_en_Colombia)). On bimodal Andisols, profiles sit near FC much of the year and refill twice. FC errors there propagate mostly into drainage, leaching and runoff partitioning, the outputs the stochastic and crop-model evidence flags as most sensitive. On unimodal Llanos Oxisols, a long dry season makes the FC–wilting-point interval the control on stress timing. A −33 kPa FC would understate available water exactly when it matters. IGAC's water-balance instruction formalises the FC-based bucket for agro-climatic work ([IGAC IN-AGR-PC08-01](https://www.igac.gov.co/sites/default/files/listadomaestro/IN-AGR-PC08-01%20V2%20Balance%20H%C3%ADdrico%20y%20sus%20Aplicaciones.pdf)). AquaCrop has been calibrated for maize at Chinchiná, Palmira and Cereté ([Agronomía Colombiana](https://revistas.unal.edu.co/index.php/agrocol/article/view/45939)). Neither the IGAC instruction nor the AquaCrop calibration tested FC definitions. No Colombian SMAP or SMOS validation network was found either, and satellite footprints are poorly matched to Andean terrain ([Casamitjana et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3390/app10165540)).

## A defensible field-capacity protocol for Colombian modelling

The evidence supports an explicit workflow rather than a single number. First, declare the definition and method for every FC value: the suction, the flux threshold, or the time after wetting, plus the sample state. Accuracy figures are only comparable within one definition. Second, match the FC definition to the model's drainage physics. For Laio-type stochastic models, derive s_fc from the same K(s) and an explicit E_max ratio, and do not substitute θ(−33 kPa), which in Clapp–Hornberger soils lands on s*. For HYDRUS, compute FC from the fitted VGM parameters at a declared flux (Twarakavi's 0.1 mm d⁻¹ is the common default) and check it against measured FC, a step Liu et al. skipped. For AquaCrop, DSSAT and APSIM buckets, calibrate FC and the drainage coefficient jointly against observed post-rain drydowns, since one-at-a-time sensitivity understates their joint effect.

Third, measure FC in ways that suit each soil order. For Andisols, use undisturbed, field-moist cores and never air-dried sieved material, with bulk density at the same moisture. Treat legacy air-dried values as lower bounds. For Oxisols, use θ(−10 kPa) from the existing AGROSAVIA curves as the default laboratory FC, and test −6 kPa and flux-based values against field drainage. Fourth, wherever sensors exist, extract FC with Bean-type cycle algorithms. Report both the knee/plateau and regression estimates, since they bracket the expert value, and keep the full cycle-to-cycle series rather than a single mean, because the variability is information about hysteresis and structure. Fifth, for regional runs on Oxisols, prefer HYBRAS-based or Hodnett–Tomasella PTFs over Rosetta or Saxton–Rawls. Acknowledge that no PTF is calibrated for allophanic soils. A Colombian hydrophysical database modelled on HYBRAS, assembled from Cenicafé, AGROSAVIA, IGAC and university archives, would fill this gap if it records sample pre-treatment.

## Conclusion

The literature has moved from asking "what is a soil's field capacity?" to asking "which drainage convention does this model need, and how stable is it?" That shift has a practical payoff. The output most sensitive to FC is not yield but the water and solute fluxes beyond the root zone: recharge, nitrate leaching and irrigation percolation. Those fluxes are also the ones hardest to observe. A modeller can calibrate a bucket to match yield and soil moisture while carrying a badly wrong FC, and the error surfaces only in the drainage term. For AGROSAVIA's mandate, which increasingly includes nutrient losses, irrigation efficiency and catchment water yield, FC deserves more attention than yield-based sensitivity analyses suggest.

The Colombian case also exposes a research opportunity. Andisols and Oxisols are both poorly served by temperate conventions, but for opposite physical reasons. Andisols retain far more water than texture predicts and lose that capacity irreversibly when dried. Oxisols hold less than their clay content predicts and drain like sands. A single national practice of −33 kPa on air-dried samples would therefore be wrong in both directions at once. Two pieces of work are missing and within reach: a paired field-moist versus air-dried retention study on Colombian Andisols, and in-situ flux-based FC tests on Altillanura Oxisols run before and after tillage. Together they would calibrate the country's legacy data, test whether pore-evolution models apply to tropical microaggregated soils, and give Colombia's stochastic, Richards and crop models a shared, physically grounded upper boundary.
